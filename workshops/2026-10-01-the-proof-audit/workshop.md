## 1. The promise

By 4:00 you will be able to say, in one sentence, whether an AI tool or automation is actually
paying off — not by how often it gets opened, but by how much faster the real work gets done. You
will leave with a budget split for next quarter's AI spending so the money doesn't disappear into
a tool nobody ends up using, and a one-page Rulebook that defines the five words your business
argues about most, with one of them turned into a rule nobody — person or tool — is allowed to
break.

## 2. What they leave with

- A written before-and-after time measurement for one piece of recurring work, so they can prove
  whether automating it actually helped.
- A budget split for next quarter's AI spending: tool cost, setup, and training.
- A one-page Rulebook: five terms their business uses every day, defined in plain English.
- One of those terms turned into a hard-stop rule, written down.

## 4. The teaching

### Idea 1 — Prove It Paid: judge AI by speed of outcome, not how much it got used

**The idea.** Measure an AI tool or automation by how much faster the real-world result shows up
— not by usage counts, token spend, or how many people are logged in.

**Where it comes from.** [snip: Measure AI By Faster Delivery](https://share.snipd.com/snip/7a497f7a-2e64-425d-9f9a-f9ee2b3e68a1)
and [snip: Token Scoreboards Created Expensive Vanity Metrics](https://share.snipd.com/snip/119e1e1a-d357-4a72-8ebc-0ddad8578530).

**What it means for a 10-person business.** The podcast's example is a software team: they stopped
tracking lines of code or tokens burned, and started tracking the time from "a bug gets reported"
to "the fix is live." Translate that into any piece of work in your business: time from invoice
overdue to payment received, time from lead to quote sent, time from application to interview
booked. If a tool doesn't shrink that number, it isn't paying off — no matter how many people say
they "use AI now."

The warning half of this is just as useful. Uber reportedly burned through its whole year's AI
token budget in thirteen weeks after the company started rewarding people for how much they used
it. Meta built a similar scoreboard. Both measured activity, not results, and got exactly what they
measured: a lot of activity. If you tell your team "use the AI tool more," you will get usage
numbers that look great and a bill that looks worse, with nothing to show for it.

**The example to use.** A bookkeeper drowning in monthly reconciliation buys a receipt-matching AI
tool. The good measure: reconciliation used to take five days of the month; now it takes one. The
bad measure: how many receipts ran through the tool this month. Only one of those numbers tells you
whether to keep paying for it.

**The common objection.** *"I don't have before-and-after numbers — I never timed this stuff."*

The honest answer: start now, roughly. Ask whoever does the work, "about how long does this take?"
Write that number down today, before you change anything. One rough number beats no number, and
it's the only way you'll ever know if the tool earned its keep.

### Idea 2 — Spend It Right: most of the AI budget goes to training, not tools

**The idea.** Split AI spending roughly 10% tool, 20% setup, 70% building the habit of actually
using it. Most AI spending fails because all of it goes to the first 30%.

**Where it comes from.** [snip: The 10-20-70 Framework For AI Budgets](https://share.snipd.com/snip/38311d3d-a9f1-4d76-a81b-cebb9cbbf477),
[snip: Spend 70% of AI Budgets on Education](https://share.snipd.com/snip/e242d4d0-7912-4483-ae79-ea8b07c9ab83),
[snip: Spend Most AI Budgets On Building Habits](https://share.snipd.com/snip/2a4f8fca-628a-4556-a839-cbeb9f351073),
and [snip: AI Tools Need Habits Before They Deliver ROI](https://share.snipd.com/snip/b3623af9-0433-4fb6-962f-d56173f7835f).

**What it means for a 10-person business.** Picture a $2,000 quarterly AI budget. Roughly $200 buys
the software. Roughly $400 pays someone — you, a freelancer, a consultant — to connect it to your
actual work. The remaining $1,400 goes to training time: paid hours for your team to practice, a
short weekly huddle where someone shows what they tried, a habit forming. The podcast's stat behind
this: across enterprises, only 8 to 9 percent of AI projects ever reach real production use. The
part that's usually missing is never the software. It's the habit.

**The example.** A shop manager buys an AI scheduling tool for $100 a month and never trains
anyone. Three employees keep using the paper calendar out of habit, because nobody showed them
anything different. Six months later, the subscription gets cancelled — money spent, nothing
changed. Now picture the same $100 a month, plus two twenty-minute trainings and one simple rule
for a month: "schedule changes go in the tool, not on paper." The adoption sticks, because the
training line in the budget wasn't zero.

**The common objection.** *"My team is stretched thin. I can't hand over 70% of a budget in
hours."*

The honest answer: the ratio matters more than the dollar amount. If your whole AI budget this
quarter is $200, "70%" might just mean one hour of your own time each week showing one person how
you actually use it. Scale the ratio to what you have. Don't skip it because the number looks
small.

### Idea 3 — Name It Once: write your rules and definitions down, and make the important ones non-negotiable

**The idea.** Decide once, in writing, what the words your business argues about actually mean.
For the handful of decisions where being wrong is expensive, write those as hard rules — not
guidelines a person or a tool might forget.

**Where it comes from.** [snip: Semantic Layers Prevent Agents From Relearning Meaning](https://share.snipd.com/snip/fff062a6-dcdd-4392-8c7a-00d291a51032),
[snip: Ontologies Add Causation To Data Meaning](https://share.snipd.com/snip/6892362e-e185-458a-9e02-0a0e73028f3f),
[snip: Design Claude Skills Around Human Workflows](https://share.snipd.com/snip/28d71975-c744-4be1-90bc-9835a3da2209),
and [snip: Keep High Stakes Decisions Inside Hard Constraints](https://share.snipd.com/snip/8df92670-c099-45c3-987e-fc6133db090f).

**What it means for a 10-person business.** The podcast's "semantic layer" is a shared glossary a
whole company's data and tools agree on, so nobody has to rediscover what a term means every time.
Translate that directly: define, once, what "VIP client" means in your business (spent over $5,000
this year?), what "overdue" means (past 30 days?), what "qualified lead" means. Skip this and every
new hire — and every new tool — decides it differently, and nothing anyone reports matches
anything else.

The podcast's advice on building AI skills breaks work into explicit steps with a person checking
the output before it goes out. Translate that: write your process down as a short numbered list
with one point in it marked "a person looks at this before it goes further." That's the whole
idea — not "the AI does everything," but a named handoff point.

And some decisions are too important to leave to judgment at all, human or automated. The
podcast's example is letting an AI explore options freely but keeping the final, high-stakes
decision inside a hard, mathematically-enforced rule it cannot talk itself out of. Translate that
into your business as a written rule somewhere a tool or an employee actually reads it, not a hope
that everyone remembers: "never discount more than 20% without approval." "Never auto-email a
client while they have an open complaint."

**The example.** A ten-person agency's AI drafts client emails. Nobody had defined "sensitive
client," so the tool once sent a routine invoice reminder to a client in the middle of an active
complaint. After: one page defining six terms, including "sensitive client," plus one hard rule —
"never auto-send to a sensitive-tagged client; always route to a person first." The fix wasn't a
smarter tool. It was writing the rule down somewhere the tool could actually see it.

**The common objection.** *"Writing all this down sounds like more work than just doing the
task."*

The honest answer: you write it once, and it holds for every future hire and every future tool.
The five-word glossary takes twenty minutes. The alternative is re-explaining the same thing to
every new employee, forever, and re-discovering the same mistake every time a new tool joins.

## 5. Live build — page one of the Rulebook, 10 minutes

**What Rachael builds on screen:** a one-page Rulebook in a plain Google Doc (any shared doc or
spreadsheet works the same way — no special software required).

- **Columns:** Term | Plain-English definition | Who decided it.
- **Row 1:** "Rush job" — ordered with less than 48 hours to delivery — Rachael.
- **Row 2:** "VIP client" — spent over $5,000 in the last 12 months — Rachael.
- **Bottom, in bold, outside the table:** "Hard rule: never discount more than 20% without
  approval."

**Visible result:** one page, five minutes of typing, that any new hire or any tool could read and
apply the same way Rachael would.

**Say while building:** "This isn't software. It's a page. The reason it works isn't the tool it
lives in — it's that everyone, and everything, reads the same page instead of guessing."

**If the demo breaks:** have the finished Rulebook page already open in a second tab. Switch to it,
say "here's one I filled in earlier," and keep going. Don't troubleshoot the doc tool live.

## 6. The exercise — The Proof Audit, 14 minutes

Handout page 1 is the Rulebook starter. Page 2 is the budget and measurement worksheet.

**The exact instruction to say out loud:**

> "Take four minutes and write down five words or phrases people in your business define
> differently depending on who you ask — things like 'urgent,' 'VIP,' 'qualified,' 'overdue.' Don't
> overthink it. Write the first five that come to mind."

Then, at the 4-minute mark:

> "Now write a one-sentence definition for each one — the definition you'd want a brand-new
> employee to use on day one. Then circle the one where getting it wrong costs you real money or a
> real relationship, and write it below as a hard rule: something nobody is allowed to decide
> case-by-case."

Then, in the remaining time:

> "Last piece. Pick one loop in your business you already pay for help with, or are thinking about
> automating. Write down your best guess at how long it takes today. That's the number you'll
> compare against in a month."

**Two examples for anyone who freezes:**

1. *A cleaning business.* Terms: "rush job," "regular client," "damage claim." Hard rule: "never
   approve a damage claim over $200 without the owner seeing photos first." Loop to time: how long
   from a client's reschedule request to a confirmed new time — today, that's about a day because
   it goes through three texts.
2. *A small accounting firm.* Terms: "high-risk return," "new client," "extension needed." Hard
   rule: "never file a return flagged high-risk without a second preparer reviewing it." Loop to
   time: how long from "client uploads documents" to "return is ready for review" — today, about
   nine days.

**While circulating, watch for two failure patterns:**

- A "definition" that's actually a feeling — "urgent means it feels urgent." Push for a number or a
  checkable fact: a dollar amount, a day count, a yes/no.
- A hard rule with no clear owner of the exception. "Never discount more than 20%" needs to say who
  can approve more. Otherwise the rule just gets quietly ignored the first time it's inconvenient.

## 7. Share-out and where this breaks — 6 minutes

**Three questions to ask the room:**

1. "Who found two people in your business who'd define the same word two different ways?" (Almost
   every hand. Normalize it — this is the whole reason the exercise exists.)
2. "Read me your hard rule." (Take three. Check that each one names who can make the exception.)
3. "What's the one number you're going to check again in a month?" (Go around fast, one sentence
   each.)

**Where this breaks — say all three of these plainly:**

- **A glossary nobody reads is worthless.** Writing the page isn't the win. Put it somewhere people
  actually open — pinned in the tool you use daily, not buried in a drive folder.
- **A budget with no real training hours doesn't change anything.** If the "70%" line is zero hours
  in practice, you've bought a tool, not a habit, and you're back where Idea 2 started.
- **A hard rule that's too strict gets worked around.** If "never discount more than 20%" has no
  fast path for the one time it's genuinely needed, people will quietly break it rather than wait.
  Build the exception path into the rule itself.

## 8. Close and the one-week assignment

**The close:**

> "You didn't come here to buy another tool. The page you just wrote down is the actual asset — the
> first time your business's own definitions exist in one place instead of in six people's heads,
> all slightly different. Tools change. The page doesn't, until you decide to change it."

**The assignment — one week, one line to write now:**

> "On the bottom of your handout, write: 'This week I will time the [loop] loop and write down what
> it costs today.' Just the measurement. Not the fix. You can't prove anything paid off until you
> know what it cost before."

## 9. Facilitator notes

**Prepare beforehand:**
- A shared Google Doc or spreadsheet, empty, ready for the live build.
- A second tab with the Rulebook example already filled in, for the demo-breaks fallback.
- Printed handouts. People write better definitions on paper than on a laptop.

**Timing risks:**
- Idea 3 is the segment to cut if running long — it has the most sub-points. Cutting it to just the
  glossary half (dropping the hard-constraint half) buys about 3 minutes without losing the
  exercise, since the exercise's hard-rule step can stand on its own with a one-line recap.
- The live build wants to run long because typing live is slow. Pre-type the column headers before
  the room arrives so only the row content gets typed live.
- Don't let the share-out turn into a discussion. One sentence each, keep moving.

**Questions that will come up:**

- *"What tool should the Rulebook live in?"* — Whatever your team already opens every day. If
  that's a shared drive, use a doc there. If it's a project tool, put it there instead. Don't add a
  new tool just to hold five definitions.
- *"How do I actually measure the 'before' time if I never tracked it?"* — Ask the person who does
  the work for their honest estimate, today, before anything changes. An estimate written down
  before you act beats a precise number you only calculate after, when you're motivated to see
  improvement.
- *"Isn't a 70% training budget just an excuse to not buy the tool?"* — No — say this directly. The
  10% and 20% still have to happen. The 70% is what makes the first 30% worth paying for at all.
- *"What if nobody agrees on the hard rule?"* — That disagreement is useful information, not a
  reason to skip it. Pick the version that protects you if you're wrong, write it down, and revisit
  it in a month once you've seen it in practice.
