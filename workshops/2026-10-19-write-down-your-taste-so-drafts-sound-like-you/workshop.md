# Write Down Your Taste So Drafts Sound Like You

## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you will turn the taste you carry in your head for one repeated creative job into a written Taste File your tools can check against, so the loop drafts in your style and you spend your time judging the draft, not rebuilding it.

Total: 25 minutes building, ~15 minutes Q&A, two minutes of close.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0-8 · The edit that worked and still was not funny + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "A creator showed a podcast host this month how one tool, Claude Code, runs three other tools, FFmpeg, Whisper, and Python, to turn phone footage into captioned short videos. FFmpeg handles the video, Whisper writes down the words, Python glues the steps together."
- "The edit worked. Captions, cuts, a finished short. And the creator's point was that the finished edit was the easy part. The hard part was teaching the system what is funny: when to cut, what to leave in, what her style actually is."
- "Here is the second story, from a demo tool this month. One agent builds a phone app feature, a second agent replays it and films it, and a person gets a draft video to judge. Two agents, one draft, one human judgment. The system did the making. The person kept the taste."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **Mechanics are not taste.** A tool can cut, caption, and assemble perfectly and still miss the joke, the pause, and the point.
2. **Taste you cannot write down, a tool cannot follow.** "Make it feel like me" is not an instruction. "Cut on the reaction, never mid-sentence, captions in short lines" is.
3. **The human job moves, it does not vanish.** You stop rebuilding every draft by hand and start judging drafts against your own written rules.

"Write the taste. Run the loop. Judge the draft."

### 8-18 · Taste is rules you can check, and the three parts of a Taste File

Teach the three parts on the board. Use any repeated creative job as the running example: a short video, a social post, a quote, a listing, a weekly email.

**Part one: timing.** "When does the work start, pause, and stop? In a video: hook in the first seconds, cut on the reaction, leave a beat before the punchline, end on the result. In a post: first line is the scene, not the announcement. Write timing as rules a checker can see, not as feelings."

**Part two: taste.** "What do you keep and what do you cut, every time? The real customer line stays, the throat-clearing goes. The mistake you fixed on camera stays if it teaches, goes if it only embarrasses. Name two keep examples and two cut examples from your own past work. Examples teach faster than adjectives."

**Part three: style.** "What makes it recognisably yours? Short captions or full sentences. Plain words or your trade's words. Your closing line, your colours, your pace. Write the never list here too: never use that phrase, never promise that thing, never show that shot."

Then the one test that makes it a file, not a mood board:

**The check test.** "Read each rule and ask: could a second person, or a tool, look at a draft and say yes or no to this rule without asking me? If not, sharpen it. 'Funny' fails the test. 'The joke lands in the first ten seconds and the caption repeats its key words' passes it. Keep sharpening until most rules pass."

*Facilitator note:* name the trap out loud. "The vague brief rarely looks lazy. It says 'engaging, on-brand, punchy' and everyone nods. Those three words have never cut a single frame. Always ask what the rule would cut, keep, or change in a real draft, and write that answer into the file."

### 18-28 · Watch it work

Work one example live. No installation for attendees: narrate and show each stage. Keep it to ten minutes. The demo follows the Snip 2 shape: Claude Code orchestrating FFmpeg, Whisper, and Python on phone footage. If video is not your room's job, run the same steps on a repeated post or email and say so plainly.

1. "Here is the job." *(A pile of phone footage from one real job, and the short video it needs to become.)* "Count the human decisions with the room: what opens, what is cut, where the captions break, what the last frame says."
2. "Here is the vague version." *(Brief: make it funny and engaging.)* "Watch the loop produce a correct, flat edit. Nothing is broken. Nothing is yours either."
3. "Here is the Taste File." *(Read three rules aloud: open on the finished result, then rewind to the start; cut every pause longer than a breath except the beat before the reveal; captions carry the spoken key words, in short lines, never covering a face.)*
4. "Here is the loop." *(Transcribe with Whisper, cut and assemble with FFmpeg and Python, draft against the three rules.)* "Notice the loop is now checking its own draft against a file it did not invent in the moment."
5. "Here is the judgment." *(Play or read both drafts. The room judges against the file, rule by rule, not by gut alone.)* "Keep, cut, or sharpen the rule. When a draft surprises you in a good way, that surprise is a new rule trying to be born. Write it down before the next run."

*Facilitator note:* if the room says writing the file takes longer than just editing, answer with the repeat math: "You pay for the file once. You pay for hand-editing every single time. This only wins on a job you do again and again, so pick a repeated job or do not build it at all."

### 28-33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you will get, and the answers:

- "My taste is hard to explain. Where do I even start?": "Start from examples, not definitions. Take three pieces you are proud of and three you wish you had cut, and write what the good ones share and what the bad ones share. That list, sharpened, is your first Taste File."
- "Will the loop just copy my old work forever?": "Only if you freeze the file. The judgment step is where taste moves: every time you overrule the file on purpose, update the file with why. The file is a living record, not a cage."
- "What if my job is not video?": "Use the same three parts. A quote has timing in its order, taste in what you include and leave out, and style in how you present it. If the job repeats and a draft can be checked, the shape fits."

### 33-58 · Build your Taste File

Everyone works solo or in pairs on their Taste File (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "Which creative job do you repeat so often you could do it half-asleep? Start there, not with a one-off."
- "Show me one good example and one cut example. What rule explains the difference?"
- "What is your timing rule? When does it start, pause, and land?"
- "What is your sharpest keep rule and your sharpest cut rule?"
- "Which style rule could a stranger check with a yes or a no?"
- "When the loop drafts, what will you judge first, and what will make you send it back?"

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their sharpest taste rule in thirty seconds. "My job is ___. My rule is ___. It would cut ___ and keep ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**What could go wrong.** "Two tales from this week. First, the polished flat draft. A loop with no Taste File produces work that is correct in every mechanical way and wrong in the only way the customer cares about, and because nothing is technically broken, no check catches it. Second, the frozen file. A team writes its taste down once, never updates it, and a year later the loop is faithfully copying a style the business has outgrown. The fix for both is the same habit: judge against the file, and update the file when your judgment changes."

### 58-60 · Wrap-up and homework

"Write the taste. Run the loop. Judge the draft."

Homework: within two weeks, run your loop three times on the same repeated job using your Taste File. Keep the three drafts. Mark each one keep, cut, or sharpen, and update the file once from what you learned. Bring back the updated file with the one rule you changed highlighted. That changed rule is the proof the system is learning your taste, not just copying your past.

*Facilitator note:* hold up a filled worksheet as you say this. "Bring this page back filled in, with your changed rule highlighted, keep or fix."

## 4. Plain-English word list

Keep this on your facilitator copy. If you are tempted to say the left column, say the right column instead, and only use the product's own word after the plain version.

| Instead of... | Say... |
|---|---|
| Taste File / skill file | Your written rules for timing, taste, and style that a tool can check a draft against |
| Orchestration | One tool running the other tools in the right order |
| Transcription | Writing down the words spoken in a recording |
| Encoding taste | Turning "I know it when I see it" into rules and examples someone else can follow |
| Loop | A job that drafts, checks against the file, revises, and hands a person the draft |
| Draft | A finished-looking first version, made to be judged, not yet sent or posted |
| Idempotency | Doing the same step twice does not do the job twice, from this week's production guide |
