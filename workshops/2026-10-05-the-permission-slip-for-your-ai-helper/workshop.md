## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you'll write the permission slip for the one AI helper your business needs most: an Authority Card that says what it may do alone, what needs your tap, and what it never touches. You'll leave with a filled-in card for that helper, plus a two-week check that tells you whether it earned more authority.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0–8 · The week the helper got a permission slip + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "This week the AI helper stopped being a question of what it can do and became a question of what it may do. A logistics company called Logistics Reply announced five pre-built warehouse agents and gave each one a written level of authority. One only watches and reports. One recommends. One proposes and waits for a yes before it writes anything into the system. One books, but only inside the rules. Same technology, different permission slips."
- "Oracle put the same idea inside its accounting software this week with something called Fusion Claw. Companies write their standard procedures, their risk limits, and who gets to decide, and the agents live inside those rules. Here's why the week mattered: eighty-five percent of big companies are trying agents, but only five percent have them in real production. Gartner says forty percent of agent projects will be canceled by 2027. Not because the models are weak. Because nobody sorted out the permission slips and the plumbing."
- "The scary number came from the sprawl story: the average big firm will run one hundred and fifty thousand agents by 2028, and ninety-seven percent of companies hit by AI breaches had no proper access controls. That's what happens when helpers multiply and nobody keeps the list. And the fix that shipped this week: TrendAI now pipes Claude's session transcripts into the compliance log. Every prompt, every answer, every tool the agent called, tied to a named user. The receipt is the product now."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **Every helper gets an authority card.** Not just a job. Three columns: what it may do alone, what needs your tap, what it never touches. The never-touches column is the most important one you will write today.
2. **Authority is a ladder, not a switch.** Rung one: observe and report. Rung two: draft for approval. Rung three: act, but only inside the box you drew. Every helper starts on rung one. It earns the next rung with two weeks of boring evidence.
3. **The card is the audit.** You write the card before the helper runs. The transcript proves what it did after. If you cannot show what a helper read and did, you cannot govern it. Your clients' version of the compliance log is your own two-week check.

"Logistics Reply runs warehouses. You run a business. The shape is the same: write down the authority, or the helper will invent it."

### 8–18 · The authority ladder

"Three rungs. That's the whole model. The warehouse agents each sit on one, and yours will too."

Draw the ladder on the board or a slide, three rungs, with the warehouse agent on each:

**Rung 1: Observe and report.** It looks, it tells you, it touches nothing. The warehouse's Out of Stock Agent watches the shelves and tells the manager what is truly missing versus what is just slow to arrive. It fixes nothing. It diagnoses. Small business version: a helper that scans your reviews on Google and Yelp each morning and reports the pattern. It never replies. It never posts. The worst it can do is a wrong morning list, and you read it with coffee.

**Rung 2: Draft for approval.** It proposes. You dispose. The warehouse's ABC Rebalancer recomputes which products belong in which storage class, then proposes the new classes. It writes nothing into the warehouse system until a human says yes. The warehouse's Labor Distribution Agent is the same shape: it recommends which workers go where today, and the manager decides. Small business version: a review-response helper that drafts a reply for every review and queues them. Nothing posts without your tap. The worst it can do unsupervised is a draft sitting in a queue.

**Rung 3: Supervised action.** It does the thing, but only inside the box you drew. The warehouse's Dock Scheduling Agent talks to drivers in plain language and books dock slots. It books real slots. But only inside the rules the warehouse set: which docks, which hours, which truck types. The human approved the rules once. The agent acts inside them. Small business version: a booking helper that fills your intake calendar inside your hours, your buffer time, and your rules. It does the booking. Only inside the box.

"Notice what the ladder does. It separates reading from doing, and it separates doing-with-permission from doing-once-a-human-said-yes-to-the-rules. Most small businesses today run helpers with no card at all. That means every helper is secretly on rung three with no box."

*Facilitator note:* if anyone asks "can a helper move down a rung," say yes, loudly. "A helper that misbehaves goes back to rung one until it earns rung two again. That's the discipline. The ladder goes both directions."

### 18–28 · Watch it work

Fill in one Authority Card live for the review-response helper. No installation for attendees: follow-along demo, or narrate the four named warehouse agents and map each one as you go. Keep it to ten minutes.

Work the example in plain words:

1. "Here's the helper." *(Write on the card: Helper name: Review Helper. Role: reads every new review, drafts a reply in my voice, queues it for my tap. Never posts.)* "A name, a job, and what it never does. That's the interview."
2. "Here's what it knows." *(Write: what it knows: the Google and Yelp review feeds, my response policy, three replies I wrote that it should sound like.)* "It reads reviews. It cannot read my email, my bank, or my calendar. The data it may read is written down. That is its contract."
3. "Here's the ladder rung." *(Write: Rung 2: draft for approval.)* "It drafts. I tap. This is the ABC Rebalancer shape: propose, wait for the yes, then act."
4. "Here's the never-touches column." *(Write: never touches: posting, deleting a review, replying to a reviewer in a private channel, promising a refund.)* "This column is the whole workshop. The warehouse's ABC Rebalancer never writes into the system without approval. Your helper never posts. Same rule."

Then ask the room: "What would you add to the never-touches column for YOUR business?" That question teaches the whole workshop: the card is personal, and the owner writes the hard line.

*Facilitator note:* if it glitches, narrate the fix: "this is what managing one looks like." Ten minutes max, then open the floor.

### 28–33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you'll get, and the answers:

- "What if I give it too little authority and it's useless?": That's the point of rung one. A watcher that saves you ten minutes every morning pays for itself. You add authority only when two weeks of evidence say the last rung was boring. Boring is the promotion criterion.
- "I already have helpers with no cards. What now?": Count them. The sprawl article says the average big firm will have one hundred and fifty thousand agents by 2028. Your version is probably six tools, two subscriptions, and a browser tab you forgot about. List them tonight. Write one card for the riskiest one. That list is your inventory, and the inventory comes before everything.
- "Should I just wait until this is more mature?": No. The shape has converged: write down the authority, keep the receipts, start low. Logistics Reply sells it to warehouses. Oracle built it into accounting software. The card you write today works for every product on the market and every one coming next year. Learn the shape now.

### 33–58 · Build your own

Everyone works solo or in pairs on the Authority Card (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "Which helper do you need most? Review replies, inbox triage, intake booking, morning summary?"
- "What may it do alone? Name three things it can do without ever asking you."
- "What needs your tap? Be specific. Sending? Spending? Promising? Posting? Deleting?"
- "Now the hard column: what does it never touch, even with your permission in the room?"

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their card in thirty seconds. "My helper is ___. May do alone: ___. Needs my tap: ___. Never touches: ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**What could go wrong.** "This week we also got the cautionary tale. Anthropic set its own AI loose to make its app faster. It shipped three thousand changes. Fresh load went from three seconds to half a second. It was a triumph by every metric they measured. And then a researcher showed the sidebar broke for users who deleted threads in another tab: the optimization cached threads that should have been gone. Faster by the number, broken in the real world. That is what an unsupervised helper does: it wins the metric and loses the business. Your never-touches column is the guardrail. Your two-week check is the inspection."

### 58–60 · Wrap-up and homework

"Every helper gets an authority card. Authority is a ladder, not a switch. The card is the audit."

Homework: fill in the Authority Card for the one helper you need most. Put it on the rung you trust it to start on, almost always rung one or two. Run it for two weeks. Bring back three things: what it did alone, what it brought you for a tap, and one item you would move to a different column. If the two weeks were boring, promote it one rung. Boring is the promotion criterion.

*Facilitator note:* hold up a filled Authority Card as you say this. "Bring this card back filled in, or better, bring the helper it describes."

## 4. Plain-English word list

Keep this on your facilitator copy. If you're tempted to say the left column, say the right column instead, and only use the product's own word after the plain version.

| Read-only access | Observe and report: it looks, it tells you, it touches nothing |
| Human-in-the-loop | Your tap: nothing important happens without it |
| Data contract | What it knows: the exact data it may read |
| Agent sprawl | Helper sprawl: helpers multiplying with no list of who is who |
| Compliance API / audit log | The receipts: what it read, what it did, tied to your name |
| Risk threshold | The line: how much can go wrong before a human must say yes |
| Decision rights | Who decides: you, every time it matters |
| Supervised action | It does the thing, but only inside the box you drew |
| Observe and diagnose | Watches and tells you what is wrong, fixes nothing |
