# A Weekly Job That Learns From Its Mistakes

## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you will take one job you repeat every week and give it a learning loop: plan, work, review, compound. You will write a one-page plan, name your reviewers, and file one real lesson from a real past mistake where the next run will find it first. You will leave with a loop drawn for your own job, a lesson file started, and the habit that makes every future run cheaper than the last one.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0-8 · The loop that learns + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "This week Claire Vo, who runs a series called How I AI, interviewed a builder named Kieran Klaassen. His system is called Compound Engineering, and the Claude Code team itself uses it. The pitch is simple: the agent gets better every time you use it, because every mistake becomes a lesson it keeps."
- "He is not talking about code magic. He is talking about a loop with four steps that any good team already knows: plan the work, do the work, review the work, and write down what you learned. The new part is where the lesson lives: not in a chat that scrolls away, and not in one person's head, but in a file the next run opens first."
- "And this week's other big story is why that file matters. A major AI tool shipped nine updates in eight days, and three of them quietly changed how people's existing safety rules behave. When the floor moves that fast, the only thing that stays yours is your written lessons: how you do the job, what went wrong last time, what to check first."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **Plan, work, review, compound.** Four steps, in that order, every time. The review is not optional and the lesson is not a diary.
2. **One lesson, filed where the next run finds it.** Short, specific, dated. One lesson per job beats a pile of vague notes.
3. **Silence is not approval.** A reviewer who says nothing has not approved anything. Name who reviews, and what a pass actually looks like.

"Plan, work, review, compound. One lesson, filed first. Silence is not approval."

### 8-18 · The four steps

Draw the loop on the board as a circle. Teach each step with the catering example: a small company that packs a Friday event every week.

**Step 1: Plan.** "Before anyone cooks, one page. What is the event, how many guests, where is it, what are the traps from last time. In the software version, the agent plans before it builds and it asks questions first. Steal that habit: make the helper, human or AI, ask three questions before it starts. What are we making, what would a bad version look like, what matters most."

**Step 2: Work.** "Do the job the normal way. The pack gets made, the invoices go out, the inbox gets answered. The loop does not change the work. It changes what happens around the work."

**Step 3: Review.** "Ten minutes while the bruises are fresh. What ran out, what was wasted, what had to be redone. In Klaassen's system, different reviewers play different roles: one plays security, one plays the careful tester who clicks every button. Your version: name two hats. The money hat asks what this cost. The customer hat asks what the customer felt. Same job, two reviews."

**Step 4: Compound.** "Write one lesson and file it where the next run starts. The catering lesson is: this venue has a tiny loading dock, leave twenty extra minutes. That line goes at the top of the Friday template, not in a notebook in a drawer. Next time that venue books, the plan already contains the lesson. The company stops paying to relearn the same fact in front of a hungry wedding party."

Then the three words people mix up, in plain form:

**"A shortcut, a recipe card, a worker."** "A saved shortcut is an instruction you fire by name. A recipe card is the context the helper pulls out only when that job shows up. A worker is someone, or some agent, you hand a piece of the job to. The loop uses all three, and each one gets better a different way: better shortcut, better recipe card, better choice of who reviews whom."

*Facilitator note:* name the trap out loud. "The loop dies two ways. One: the review does not happen, so nothing compounds and you just have a plan and some work, which is what you already had. Two: the lesson file becomes a long diary nobody can use. Keep lessons short, specific, and dated, or the next run cannot trust them."

### 18-28 · Watch it work

Loop one real job live. No installation for attendees: follow-along demo, or narrate and map each step as you go. Keep it to ten minutes.

Work the example in plain words:

1. "Here is the repeated job." *(Show a messy Friday event pack from a past week, with one known mistake in it.)* "Every business has this job. It happens every week, it is done from memory, and the same two mistakes keep visiting."
2. "Here is the plan page." *(Write it on the board, reading aloud.)* "Event, guests, venue, traps from last time. Three questions answered before any work starts."
3. "Here is the work, skipped." *(Point at the finished pack.)* "We are not cooking today. The loop lives around the work, not inside it."
4. "Here is the review, in two hats." *(Money hat: what was wasted. Customer hat: where did the client wait.)* "Ten minutes, two hats, three findings max."
5. "Here is the compound step." *(Write the one lesson at the top of next week's template, in front of the room: venue dock, twenty extra minutes, dated today.)* "That line is the whole system. Next run, the plan starts smarter because last run left a note at the exact place the next run begins."

*Facilitator note:* if the room says their jobs are all different every week, celebrate the partial truth: "the jobs differ, the traps repeat. Compound the traps."

### 28-33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you will get, and the answers:

- "Is this just a checklist with a new name?": "A checklist is step two insurance. The loop adds the two steps checklists usually miss: a plan written before the work, and a lesson filed after the review that changes next week's checklist. If your checklist never changes, you have a checklist. If it grows one good line after every run, you have a compound loop."
- "What if I work alone? Who reviews?": "You do, in a named hat, on a timer, before you file the work away. Ten minutes in the money hat is a different review than zero minutes in no hat. And if you use an AI helper, give it the other hat: you wear money, it wears customer, and it has to name one problem before it is allowed to say the work looks good."
- "How long until this pays off?": "By the fourth run of the same job. The first run builds the plan, the second tests it, the third catches a repeated trap before it costs you, and that catch is the payoff made visible. Keep the lessons short and the payoff arrives on schedule."

### 33-58 · Draw your loop

Everyone works solo or in pairs on their loop plan (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "What is the job that repeats? Name the weekly or monthly job you could do in your sleep, and occasionally do badly for exactly that reason."
- "What goes on the plan page? Event, numbers, and the traps from last time, in five lines or fewer."
- "What are your three questions first? The questions the helper must ask before it starts."
- "Who reviews, in which hats? Name the money hat and the customer hat, even if both are you."
- "What is your one lesson from the last real run? Short, specific, dated, and written at the top of the place the next run starts."
- "What does a pass look like? Write what approval actually means, because silence is not approval."

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their one lesson in thirty seconds. "My job is ___. My lesson from last time is ___. It now lives at the top of ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**What could go wrong.** "Two tales from the pattern. First, the diary. A team filed a lesson after every job, pages and pages, until the file was so long that nobody and no agent could find the useful lines, and the loop quietly stopped working. The fix is the rule: one lesson per job, short, specific, dated, and old lessons get folded into the recipe card or deleted. Second, the polite reviewer. An agent reviewed its own work, said it looked great, and shipped a mistake to a customer, because nobody told it that silence and praise are not a review. The fix is the pass rule: the reviewer must name one risk before it can approve, and a human owns the final yes on anything customer facing."

### 58-60 · Wrap-up and homework

"Plan, work, review, compound. One lesson, filed first. Silence is not approval."

Homework: run your loop four times on the same job in the next month. Each time, spend ten minutes on the review and file exactly one lesson where the next run starts. Bring back three things: your plan page, your four lessons, and the first trap the loop caught before it cost you money. If all four lessons are boring, the loop is working.

*Facilitator note:* hold up a filled worksheet as you say this. "Bring this page back filled in, or better, bring the template your lessons now live on."

## 4. Plain-English word list

Keep this on your facilitator copy. If you are tempted to say the left column, say the right column instead, and only use the product's own word after the plain version.

| Hooks | Your safety rules: the checks that run when something happens |
