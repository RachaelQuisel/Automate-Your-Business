## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you'll put an AI helper to work inside the one document you live in. You'll give it one editing job with clear boundaries, set it to ask before it edits, review its proposed edits together, and write the prenup. You'll leave with a helper working where the work is, and the discipline to keep it honest.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0–8 · The week the commute ended + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "For years the pattern was this. You had your document, and you had a chat box, and you were the commuter. Copy the paragraph, paste it into the AI, copy the answer, paste it back, fix the formatting it broke. Millions of people did this every day. It was the defining interaction of the AI era, and it was terrible. It was a copy-paste commute."
- "This week the commute ended. Anthropic put Claude directly inside Google Docs, Sheets, and Slides. Open your spreadsheet and Claude is in the sidebar. It reads the cells, it writes formulas, it builds pivot tables, it creates new tabs. For messy jobs it pulls your data into Python, does the work, and writes the answer back into the sheet. It does not give you a formula to copy. It does the job in the file."
- "And here is the part that matters more than the feature. It defaults to Ask before edits. Every change shows up as a proposed edit you review before applying. There is also an Accept all edits mode. That button is the most dangerous button in your business software this year, and we will talk about why you never touch it on a live file."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **Work where the work is.** The agent comes to your document. You stop commuting with copy-paste. The document becomes the desk you both sit at.
2. **Ask before it edits.** The proposed-edits sidebar is not a formality. It is the work. The review is the job, and the fortieth proposed edit deserves the same attention as the first.
3. **The prenup goes on the back.** An agent inside every spreadsheet is an embedded agent, and removing an embedded agent is painful. Write the exit terms before you embed: how your data gets out, what the off-ramp is.

"Work where the work is. Ask before it edits. Prenup on the back."

### 8–18 · The permission model

"This is the part of the hour that will save you from the worst day of your year. The permission model."

Draw two columns on the board:

**Column 1: Ask before edits (the default).** "Claude shows you every change before it lands. In Docs, big revisions appear as proposed edits in the sidebar. You read them, you tap accept or reject, per change. In Sheets, the formula fixes and the new tabs are proposed, not applied. You are the approver. This is yesterday's Authority Card expressed as a product setting: it may draft alone, it needs your tap to change anything."

**Column 2: Accept all edits (the mode you never touch on a live file).** "One click and it does the whole job without stopping. Sounds great. Here is what it costs. Review fatigue is real. The fortieth proposed edit gets less attention than the first, and that is a human problem, not a model problem. Accept-all on the live revenue sheet on a Friday afternoon is how wrong numbers end up in front of the board. The rule: Accept-all is for a sandbox copy on a Sunday afternoon when you have backups. Never the live file."

Then the third point, the one nobody teaches:

**"The new failure shape is not a rogue agent. It is a diligent agent executing a stale workflow with a tired approver."** Tell the board-deck story: "Claude builds twelve slides from your sales data and your notes. Eleven are perfect. Slide seven has last quarter's numbers, because the saved workflow pulled from a stale export nobody deleted. You reviewed the proposed edits at midnight. You clicked accept on all of them. The wrong numbers go to the board. Everybody followed the process and the outcome is still wrong."

"So the Skill, the saved workflow, needs an owner and a last-reviewed date, like any other business process. Your quarterly-deck workflow should have a line: data sources verified, export dates checked, owner initials. The agent does not get tired. You do. Build the process for the tired version of yourself."

*Facilitator note:* if anyone says "this sounds like more work, not less," agree, then reframe: "The review is the job. Ten minutes of review replaces four hours of doing. That is still a bargain. But the ten minutes are not optional."

### 18–28 · Watch it work

Run one spreadsheet job live. The monthly close is the canonical demo: every owner in the room has one. No installation for attendees: follow-along demo, or narrate and map each part as you go. Keep it to ten minutes.

Work the example in plain words:

1. "Here's the document I live in." *(Open a sample month-end spreadsheet.)* "One month of transactions. This is the desk."
2. "Here's the one job." *(Say it in one breath: "Reconcile this month's transactions against the bank export, flag anything that doesn't match, and write a plain-English summary of the month.")* "One job. One breath. If it takes two breaths, it is two jobs."
3. "Here's the boundary." *(Set Ask before edits on screen. Say: "It may read everything and draft everything alone. It needs my tap before any cell changes. It never touches the bank login, the payroll tab, or anything outside this sheet.")* "Same three columns as the Authority Card. The discipline carries over."
4. "Here's the review." *(Walk through the proposed edits in the sidebar. Accept three, reject one on purpose. Narrate the rejection: "This is the stale-export problem. The data was old, the edit was confident, and the review caught it. This is the whole workshop in one click.")*

Then ask the room: "What is the one document YOU live in? Not the perfect document. The one you open every week and sigh at." That question teaches the whole workshop: the helper goes where the sigh is.

*Facilitator note:* if it glitches, narrate the fix: "this is what managing one looks like." Ten minutes max, then open the floor.

### 28–33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you'll get, and the answers:

- "What if I use Microsoft instead of Google?": "Same story, other desk. Claude already lives inside Word, Excel, and PowerPoint, generally available on paid plans, with Outlook in beta. The pattern is identical: sidebar, proposed edits, ask-before-edits default. Pick the desk you already sit at. Do not switch ecosystems for the agent."
- "Do I need the paid plan for this?": "Yes, this one needs a paid Claude plan, and that is actually useful information. Google just moved its free Gemini users down to the basic model on October 9th, and OpenAI is halving the Pro usage bucket on October 30th. The free tier is shrinking everywhere. Budget for the tool the way you budget for any employee: the paid seat is the one that does the work."
- "What about my data? Is Claude reading everything?": "It reads what you point it at, and access follows your existing sharing permissions. The enterprise version carries the Compliance API and audit exports, so there is a record of what it touched. But the real answer is the prenup, which you are about to write: know how your data gets out before you put more of it in."

### 33–58 · Put your helper in your document

Everyone works solo or in pairs on their document plan (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "Which document do you live in? The spreadsheet, the doc, the deck. Name it."
- "What is the one job? Say it in one breath."
- "Where are the boundaries? May do alone, needs your tap, never touches. Write all three."
- "Now the review plan. When will you review the proposed edits, and what will you check first? The fortieth edit gets the same attention as the first. How will you make that true?"
- "And the prenup. If you had to pull this helper out tomorrow, how painful? Write the exit line."

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their plan in thirty seconds. "My document is ___. The one job is ___. It may ___, it needs my tap for ___, it never touches ___. I will review proposed edits ___, and my prenup is ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**What could go wrong.** "Two tales from this week. First, the stale-export board deck. Diligent agent, stale workflow, tired approver, wrong numbers in front of the board. The fix is the review discipline and the last-reviewed date on the saved workflow. Second, the Accept-all Friday. Somebody's intern clicks Accept all edits on the live revenue sheet at 4 PM on a Friday. The fix is the rule: Accept-all is for sandbox copies on Sunday afternoons with backups. Never the live file. The product sets the default. You keep the discipline."

### 58–60 · Wrap-up and homework

"Work where the work is. Ask before it edits. The prenup goes on the back."

Homework: put the helper in your document this week. One document, one job, ask-before-edits on, review every proposed edit the first three times it runs. Bring back three things: what the review caught, what the fortieth edit felt like, and one line you'd add to the prenup. If the first run was boring, the job description was good. Boring is still the promotion criterion.

*Facilitator note:* hold up a filled worksheet as you say this. "Bring this page back filled in, or better, bring the document it describes."
