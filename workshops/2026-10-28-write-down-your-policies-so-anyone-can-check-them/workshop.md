# Write Down Your Policies So Anyone Can Check Them

## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you will write down the five policies your customers argue about most, each one as a rule anyone can quote, the reason for the rule in one sentence, and the date you last checked it, all in one place. You will then test that page the way a customer now tests it, by asking your own AI helper the hard questions and fixing every answer that contradicts what your team would actually say. You will leave with one true version of your rules, a short list of the challenges your page already fails, and a response habit your whole team can use: cite the policy, stand behind it or name the exception, and log the challenge.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0-8 · The customer who read your policy more recently than you did + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "On October 27, 2026, Capacity, a company that sells customer service automation, released a survey of 1,000 consumers in the United States. Thirty five percent said they have used an AI agent to handle a customer service request at least once. That share is 49 percent among Gen Z and Millennial respondents. Fifty five percent have used an AI tool to help with a request. Forty four percent said the AI contradicted a company representative or the company policy. And when the customer's AI and the company representative disagree, consumers said they are equally likely to trust either one."
- "Capacity sells customer service automation, so read its framing as a vendor's. The behavior is still the behavior, and any business in this room will meet it. Thirty six percent said they would use AI to research return, refund, or cancellation policies before contacting a business. Thirty four percent said AI makes them ask more specific questions. Twenty three percent said they would ask the representative to explain or cite the company policy."
- "So here is the question for this hour, and it is not about robots. If a customer, or their helper, asked you to cite your policy right now, could you? The same words on your website, in your confirmation message, and out of your newest team member's mouth? If those three differ, a customer's helper will find the difference in seconds, because finding differences between documents is what it is good at. This workshop is how you find the difference first, on a quiet day, and fix the sentence."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **One rule, one true version, in one place.** If a policy lives in three places with three wordings, you do not have a policy. You have a dispute waiting for a reader patient enough to compare them. That reader now exists and works for your customer.
2. **Write every rule so a stranger can quote it.** A rule, the reason in one sentence, and the date it was last checked. A rule with a reason survives an argument better than a rule alone, and a checked date tells you whether you are quoting a current rule or an old one.
3. **Test your page the way a customer tests it, before a customer does.** Ask your own helper the hard questions. Every answer that contradicts what your team would do is a problem found for free, in private, instead of in a dispute, in public.

"Collect. Write to be quoted. Test. Respond the same way every time."

### 8-18 · The four steps, the five policies, and the response habit

Teach the four steps on the board. Use a home services and rental care business with nine staff as the running example. The business cleans houses, turns over a small number of rentals between guests, and sends people out for small repairs and checks.

**Step one: collect the five policies customers argue about most.** "Do not start with every rule you have. Start with five: refunds, cancellations, late arrival or late delivery, what happens when something goes wrong during the service, and fees that surprise people. Your five may differ by one or two. Name yours on the handout. For each one, gather every place the rule is currently written: the website, the confirmation message, the terms a customer accepts, the answer your team gives from memory. Put them side by side. The differences you find in this step are the workshop's raw material, and finding three wordings for one rule is normal, not shameful."

**Step two: write each policy to be quoted.** "Each policy gets exactly three parts. The rule, in one or two sentences a stranger could quote back to you, with numbers spelled as numbers: the amount, the hours, the days. The reason, in one sentence, in plain words, because 'our cleaners need the full time between guests to clean every room' ends more arguments than 'policy is policy.' And the date last checked, which a named person updates every time the rule is reviewed. Write the final text once, in the one place, and copy that exact text everywhere else it appears. Never retype it. Retyping is where versions are born."

**The exception rule.** "Teach this slowly, because it is where good businesses quietly lose their policies. Every exception you write in a message becomes a document a future customer's helper can quote. Making an exception is often the kind and correct call. The discipline is to name it as one, in the message itself: this is an exception because of these circumstances, and it does not change the rule. Then log it, dated, with the reason. If the same exception happens nine times, it is not an exception anymore. It is your actual policy, and the page should say so before a customer's helper says it for you."

**Step three: test the page with your own helper.** "Open whatever AI helper you already use. Be your own worst customer for twenty minutes. Ask it to build the strongest case against each fee and each deadline using only your published words. Ask the questions customers actually ask: can I get a refund if I cancel the day before, what happens if I arrive late, is there a fee for this, where does it say that. Where the helper wins, your words lose, and you rewrite the sentence, not the helper. Keep the questions it used. That list becomes training for new staff, who can practice against it before they meet the real version."

**Step four: set the response habit.** "When a challenge comes, and it will, the team does three things, in order. Cite the policy: name the rule and where it is written, in the words on the page. Stand behind it, or name the exception: if you are making an exception, say that it is one and why, on purpose, not as a scramble. Log the challenge: if it exposed a real contradiction, write it down before the end of the day, with the customer's question and the two wordings that conflicted. A monthly half hour reviews that log, rewrites the rule reality keeps bending, and updates the checked date. Cite, name, log. That is the whole habit."

**The approval and start rules that travel with this workshop.** "Today's research carries the matching rules for the tools themselves, and they belong in the same notebook. A tested guide to building AI skills sorts every job by how it is allowed to start: some jobs start when asked, some only a person may start, some are background rules that are simply always in the room, and some run in a helper and return only a summary. A phone and agent product gathered the same decision into a permissions page with two lists, Always Ask and Allow without Asking. And a marketing platform that sped campaign setup from weeks to under an hour still routes every output to a human signature, which teaches the limit: speeding up the work does not add approvers, so count the approvals per day a new tool will ask of you before you adopt it. Your policy page needs the same treatment. Decide which answers any team member may give, which need the owner, and which are never given without checking the page. Write that down too."

### 18-28 · Watch it work

Work one example live. Use the same home services and rental care business. Keep it to ten minutes.

1. "Here is the dispute without one true version." *(A guest cancels two days before arrival. The website says the cancellation fee applies inside seven days. The confirmation message says a fee may apply to late cancellations. The person answering remembers that last season the owner waived the fee for a family with a sick child and told them in a message that 'we are flexible when life happens.' The guest's helper gathers all three, quotes the friendliest two, and asks why the fee applies to them. The business spends four messages defending three documents against each other and waives the fee anyway, tired. Nobody lied. Nobody had one policy.)* "The business lost a fee it was entitled to, and the guest still left annoyed, because the argument itself felt unfair."
2. "Here is the same business after the four steps." *(The cancellation rule now reads, in one place: if you cancel inside seven days of arrival, the first night and the cleaning fee are charged, because the house is usually held off the market for weeks and a late opening rarely rebooks. Checked on a named date. That exact text is on the website, in the confirmation, and in the answer template. The same guest cancels, the helper quotes the page, and the answer is one message: here is the rule, here is the line, here is why it exists. The guest is disappointed and the conversation is over in two messages, with no one's honesty in question.)* "Same rule. Same fee. Completely different dispute, because there was only one text to argue about, and it had a reason in it."
3. "Here is the test catching what the team could not see." *(During the workshop test, the owner asks her own helper whether a guest can check in early if the house is empty the night before. The helper answers yes, quoting an old line on a frequently asked questions page that nobody had updated since the cleaning schedule changed. The team's real rule is no, because cleaning takes the full time between guests. One sentence on an old page had been promising what the operation cannot deliver, to every customer whose helper read that page. It is fixed in the session, in the one place, and copied exactly to the other two homes of that text.)* "That is the test paying for the whole workshop in one find."
4. "Here is the exception, named." *(A long standing customer asks to move a cleaning visit by one day in a week when the crew genuinely has room. The owner agrees, and writes: we are making an exception this once because the crew has an open slot that day; the regular rule, visits move only with seven days notice, still stands. The exception goes in the log with its date and reason. Three months later, another customer's helper finds the message history the customer shares and argues for the same move. The answer cites the rule and the named exception, and holds.)* "Kindness, documented as kindness, does not become a loophole."
5. "Here is the log changing a rule on purpose." *(The monthly review finds the same exception four times: customers asking to pay a fee in two parts after a large repair. The owner decides reality is right, rewrites the policy to allow two payments above a named amount, updates the checked date, and copies the new text everywhere.)* "That is a policy improving in the open, by the owner's decision, instead of drifting in private, one tired message at a time."

*Facilitator note:* if the room says their customers do not use AI yet, answer with the age split in the survey: "Half of customers under forty already have. The habit this workshop builds, one true version, serves every customer, including the one with only a phone and a question. You are not preparing for robots. You are preparing to be quoted accurately."

### 28-33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you will get, and the answers:

- "Is one page not too simple for a real business?": "One place does not mean one short page. It means one source. Your source can be a long document with a section per policy, as long as every other place copies its text exactly and points back to it. The test is simple: if you change the rule, how many places must you edit before the business is telling one story again? If the answer is more than one edit copied exactly, you have work to do in step one."
- "What if a customer's helper is just wrong about our policy?": "Then the page is your friend. Answer with the exact line and where it lives, kindly, once. A wrong claim about a clear rule is a short conversation. Save your energy for the claims that are right, because those are the ones that change your page, and be grateful they arrived as a message instead of a public review."
- "How often do we have to recheck the page?": "Every time a rule changes, the day it changes, and a full read once a month against the challenge log. The checked date is per policy, not per page, so a rule you reviewed last week and a rule nobody has read in a year do not wear the same date. If a policy's checked date is older than your last price change, read that one first."

### 33-58 · Write and test your own five policies

Everyone works solo or in pairs on their own five policies (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "Read me your five. Are these really the five your customers argue about, or the five that were easiest to find?"
- "Read this rule out loud. Could a stranger quote it back and know the amount, the timing, and what happens next?"
- "Where is the reason? Say it in one sentence without using the words policy or process."
- "Show me the three places this rule currently lives. Which wording wins today, and why that one?"
- "Read the hardest customer question for this rule. Now ask your helper to answer it from your published words only. Where does its answer differ from what your team would do?"
- "Read an exception you have made in the last year. Was it named as an exception at the time? Write the naming sentence now."
- "Which answers may any team member give, which need you, and which are never given without the page open? Write the three lists."
- "What is the checked date on your oldest policy, honestly? Put that policy first in your monthly review."

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their clearest rule and the one contradiction their test found, in thirty seconds. "Our rule is ___. Our test found ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**The failure gallery.** "The business with three refund rules, each written by a different person in a different year, meeting the first customer patient enough to compare them. The exception granted warmly in a late night message that became next season's quoted policy. The frequently asked questions page from two operations ago, promising early arrival the current cleaning schedule cannot deliver, read faithfully by every customer's helper. The representative arguing from memory against a customer quoting the website, both of them right about different documents. The monthly log nobody kept, so the same contradiction was discovered by four customers in a row. Every one of these is prevented by a rule you used today: one source, a rule written to be quoted, a reason, a checked date, a named exception, and a test you run before your customers run it for you."

### 58-60 · Wrap-up and homework

Close in two minutes:

1. Restate the promise kept: "You now hold five policies in one place, each with a rule a stranger can quote, a reason in one sentence, and a checked date, plus a list of the contradictions your own test found and the three lists for who may answer what."
2. Homework (say it exactly): "This week, copy the exact text of your five policies to every place they appear, website, confirmation messages, and answer templates, without retyping. Run the hard questions test once more with a team member watching. Start the challenge log with today's findings, and put a monthly half hour on the calendar to review it. Bring one dispute from the log, and the sentence you rewrote because of it, to your next session."
3. Final line: "Your customers' helpers are going to read your rules carefully, whether or not you ever do. Read them first, write them once, and let one true version do the arguing for you."

## Facilitator notes

- Keep all language plain. "Policy" may be used only with a concrete example, as it is given in this plan and on the handout.
- Do not present any product named in the news as approved or recommended. The Capacity survey is a vendor survey of 1,000 consumers in the United States, and it should be described that way, with the sample size named. The writing, testing, and logging habits are the deliverable; the products are the examples.
- Treat every vendor number in the source stories as a claim the maker or the vendor made about itself, and say so when you use one. The Sprinklr setup time is the company's figure, and the survey percentages are Capacity's published results.
- Time discipline: protect the 25-minute build block. Cut Q&A before cutting building time.
