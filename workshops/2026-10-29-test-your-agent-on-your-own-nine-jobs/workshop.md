# Test Your Agent on Your Own Nine Jobs

**Title:** Test Your Agent on Your Own Nine Jobs
**Source:** Soft Fork snips, 2026-10-29: Snip 1 (How I AI personal-agent benchmark, Claire Vo, 2026-10-28), Snip 2 (Gemini 4 Carbon and Zapier AutomationBench at 53.9 percent, The Daily Fledgling, 2026-10-29), Snip 3 (Surge AI DAYJOB: Tax, best pass rate 10.8 percent at a 95 percent rubric, 2026-10-27), Snip 4 (BuiltOnAir S26-E04, managing Airtable production changes, 2026-10-27), Snip 8 (Android Studio Change Review, DEV, 2026-10-29)

**Audience:** Small business owners and operators: no technical background assumed

## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you will choose the nine jobs you would actually give an AI helper, taken from your own last week of work, and you will write down, for each job, what a good result looks like in a short checklist anyone could score from. You will leave with your own test, ready to run on any agent, a scoring sheet with two columns (correctness, scored from your checklist by someone or something that did not do the work, and taste, scored by you in words), and a decision page with three lists: which jobs an agent may do for work, which it may do for life and admin, and which jobs no agent gets yet.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0-8 · Four agents, nine jobs, one winner by a lot, and why her test is not your test + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "On October 28, 2026, Claire Vo, who hosts the show How I AI, published the first How I AI personal-agent benchmark. She said she has personal-agent fatigue. She took four personal agents, Grok Bot, Dot from ChatGPT, Muse from Meta, and Claude Duet, and gave all four the same nine real tasks: draft a Slack reply in her voice, cut a short video from raw footage, fix a bug from a crash report, build a thumbnail in a design app, triage an inbox for to-dos, build a family newspaper, explain a physics homework question to a fourth grader, shop for shoes, and make a launch video for her own product. Other agents operated the contestants, one agent scored the results live, and Grok Bot came first, in the words of the next morning's report, by a lot."
- "Two other grades landed the same week. On Zapier's test of everyday business automation tasks, a new Google model, Gemini 4 Carbon, finished 53.9 percent, the best score so far. That means the champion fails nearly half of everyday office work. On Surge AI's test of 50 real tax assignments, with 1,789 client files, the best agent passed 10.8 percent, where passing meant meeting at least 95 percent of a written checklist for the job, including problems the client never mentioned."
- "So here is the question for this hour. None of those grades is a grade for your business. Her nine jobs are her jobs. The tax files are not your files. This workshop builds the only grade that transfers: your nine jobs, your material, your checklist, your scoreboard."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **Same jobs, same material, same wording, for every candidate.** If each agent gets different work, the difference you see belongs to the work, not the agent. Holding the jobs still is the whole test.
2. **Write what good looks like before anyone runs.** A short checklist, three to five lines per job, written in advance. If you cannot write what good means for a job, that job is not ready for an agent at all, and finding that out is a result, not a failure.
3. **Score correctness and taste separately, and never let the worker hold the pen.** Correctness is scored from the checklist by someone or something that did not do the work. Taste is scored by you, in words, because a thumbnail or a reply in your voice can pass every checklist line and still not sound like you.

"Pick nine. Save the real material. Write good first. Run them the same. Score in two columns. Sign the decision."

### 8-18 · The six steps, the nine jobs, and the three lists

Teach the six steps on the board. Use a home services and rental care business with nine staff as the running example. The business cleans houses, turns over a small number of rentals between guests, and sends people out for small repairs and checks.

**Step one: pick the nine jobs from last week, not from your imagination.** "Open last week's calendar and your sent mail. The jobs you actually did are in there. Pick nine that repeat, that hurt when they go wrong, or that cost money. For the example business, a fair nine would be: answer a guest's question about arrival, turn a booking message into a cleaning assignment, check whether tomorrow's cleaning has an accepted cleaner, draft a reply when a guest reports something broken, sort the morning inbox into jobs and not jobs, write the short note that goes with an invoice, summarise a supplier's change of terms, prepare the list of checks for a property visit, and draft the message that asks a past customer for a review. Your nine will differ. That is the point."

**Step two: save the real material for each job.** "For each job, keep the real material the agent would see: the real message thread, the real booking details, the real supplier email, with private names removed where a name is private. A test built on tidy made-up material grades the agent on a world that does not exist. Claire Vo used her real footage and her real product launch. Use your real week."

**Step three: write what good looks like, before anyone runs.** "Three to five lines per job. For answering a guest's arrival question, good might read: the arrival time stated is the real arrival time, the answer matches the one written policy, no early arrival is promised that cleaning cannot deliver, and the tone is the tone of our own last twenty replies. For sorting the inbox, good means every real job in the inbox is found, and no job is invented that is not in there. Write the lines while nobody's score depends on them. A checklist written after you see the answers will quietly bend toward whoever won."

**The scorer rule.** "Teach this slowly. The agent being graded never scores itself, and the person who ran a job does not mark their own favourite's answers from memory. Use a second person, or a second agent from a different company than the one being tested, because a scorer from the same family tends to share the builder's blind spots. Claire Vo's test used operators and a scorer that were not the contestants. Copy that separation even in a two person business: swap scoring with a partner, or score each other's runs from the written checklist only."

**The two secret jobs.** "Keep two of your jobs private and never publish or discuss them in a demo or a post. Run them alongside the nine. If a candidate aces the well-known jobs and fails your two private ones, you have learned something no public scoreboard will tell you, because a test everyone can study for stops measuring the job and starts measuring the studying."

**Step four: run every candidate the same way.** "Same material, same wording, same order if you can manage it, and no rescuing anyone mid job. The urge to help your favourite with a better follow-up question is overwhelming and completely human. Resist it. A rescued score is a wrong score you paid for."

**Step five: score in two columns.** "Correctness first, from the checklist, line by line, pass or fail per line, no half marks invented on the spot. Then taste, in your own words: this reply sounds like us, that one sounds like a hotel chain; this summary is clear, that one is clear and cold. Write the taste words down the day you score. Taste that is not written down is forgotten within a month, and then the next test starts from feelings again."

**Step six: sign the decision page.** "Three lists. Jobs this agent may do for work, with the checks that stay in place. Jobs it may do for life and admin, where a mistake costs less. And jobs no agent gets yet, with the reason in one line, for example: this job needs someone to notice a problem nobody mentioned, and today's best score on that kind of noticing, in the tax test, was about one job in ten. Put a retest date on the page. A new contestant arrives every few weeks. With the jobs, the material, and the checklist kept, retesting is an afternoon. Without them, every launch restarts the argument."

**The numbers that travel with this workshop.** "Carry the two grades from this week as your sense of scale. Fifty three point nine percent is the current champion's score on everyday business automation tasks, so plan the other forty six point one percent on purpose: which jobs get a second checker, which get a human glance, which are not automated until the score moves. Ten point eight percent is the best score on full professional assignments with messy files, so on that kind of job the agent is a first pair of hands and never the last pair of eyes. Neither number grades your nine jobs. They tell you what range to expect, and why the decision page needs a third list."

### 18-28 · Watch it work

Work one example live. Use the same home services and rental care business. Keep it to ten minutes.

1. "Here is choosing without a test." *(The owner tries one agent for a week in March on whatever comes up, likes its tone, moves the guest messages to it, then tries a second agent in May during a quiet week and cannot tell which is better, because March and May were different businesses. In June a guest is promised an arrival time cleaning cannot meet. Nobody can say which agent was better at arrival answers, because arrival answers were never tested on their own.)* "No scoreboard, no checklist, and a promise the operation could not keep. That is the normal way businesses choose, and it produces exactly this."
2. "Here is the same business with its nine jobs written." *(The nine jobs from step one are listed, the real material is saved with guest names removed, and each job has its good checklist. The arrival job's checklist includes the line: no early arrival promised that cleaning cannot deliver. That line exists because the owner wrote it in a quiet room in October, not because a guest complained in June.)* "The checklist is the business's own rules, written before the contest, where they can still protect someone."
3. "Here is the run catching what tone would have missed." *(Two agents answer the arrival question. Both sound warm and competent. On the checklist, one promises that an early arrival will probably be fine if the house is empty, which fails the named line, because the house being empty does not mean it is cleaned. The other states the real arrival time and offers the luggage suggestion the policy names. Tone said tie. The checklist said clear winner, on the one line that prevents a bad stay.)* "This is why correctness is scored from the sheet, not from how the answer feels."
4. "Here is the job both agents failed, and why that is the best result of the day." *(On the job of turning a supplier's change of terms into a summary, both agents repeat the new price and miss that the notice period for cancelling the service shrank from thirty days to seven, which was the consequential line in the email. The job goes on the third list, no agent gets it yet, with the reason written in one line: both missed the one change that costs money.)* "A failed job, found in a test, costs an afternoon. The same failure found in a contract costs a season."
5. "Here is the decision page, signed." *(Work list: inbox sorting and the invoice note, each with a human glance at the exceptions. Life and admin list: the review request draft and the visit checklist. No agent yet list: supplier terms summaries and any reply that reports something broken, because those need a person to notice what was not said. Retest date: the first week of January, or when a new agent the owner is seriously considering appears, whichever comes first.)* "Three lists, one date, one signature. That page ends more launch arguments than any opinion in the building."

*Facilitator note:* if the room says they only use one agent and do not plan to switch, answer with the retest point: "The test is not only for switching. It tells you which of your jobs your current agent should stop doing, which is the decision most owners actually need. A scoreboard with one contestant is still a scoreboard."

### 28-33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you will get, and the answers:

- "Nine jobs feels like a lot of work to set up.": "It is one afternoon, and most of it is work you already own: last week's calendar, the real messages, and the rules you already run by, written as checklist lines. Compare it with the cost of choosing wrong for a year, or with re-deciding at every launch. The setup is the cheap part. Guessing repeatedly is the expensive part."
- "What if my favourite agent loses?": "Then the test just paid for itself. You have three honest options: give it the jobs it won and another agent the jobs it lost, keep it and accept the weak jobs with a human checker named, or wait and retest on the date on your page. All three are decisions. What the test removes is the fourth option, not knowing."
- "Can I just use Claire Vo's nine jobs or the published tests?": "Use them as a starting list for choosing your own, not as your test. Her jobs came from her week, a video show and a software product. Published tests use other people's files and other people's pass marks. The tax test passed an agent at 95 percent of a professional checklist. Your pass mark for a guest message might be every line, because one wrong promise is the whole failure. Your jobs, your material, your mark."

### 33-58 · Build and score your own nine jobs test

Everyone works solo or in pairs on their own test (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "Read me your nine. Are these the jobs from last week, or the jobs you wish your week looked like?"
- "Show me the real material for this job. What did you remove for privacy, and does the job still make sense without it?"
- "Read this job's good checklist out loud. Could a stranger score an answer from these lines without asking you what you meant?"
- "Which line in this checklist prevents the most expensive mistake? Say the mistake out loud."
- "Who scores correctness for this job, and why is that scorer not the agent and not the person who ran it?"
- "Where are your two private jobs? Do not tell me what they are. Just show me they exist and are written down."
- "Read your taste words for one answer. Did you write what you liked, or only that you liked it?"
- "Show me your third list, the jobs no agent gets yet. Read the one-line reason for each. Is the reason about the job, or about your feelings about the agent?"
- "What is your retest date, and what event would make you retest sooner?"

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their hardest job and the one checklist line that decides it, in thirty seconds. "Our hardest job is ___. The line that decides it is ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**The failure gallery.** "The owner who chose by tone and learned about the arrival promise from an unhappy guest. The test built on tidy sample material that every agent passed and real material that none did. The checklist written after the answers arrived, bent so kindly toward the favourite that it graded nothing. The agent that scored its own homework and reported a wonderful year. The taste verdict nobody wrote down, argued again from memory at every launch. The public test studied so hard by every vendor that the scores rose while the work did not. Every one of these is prevented by a rule you used today: real jobs, real material, the checklist written first, a scorer who is not the worker, taste in words, and two jobs kept private."

### 58-60 · Wrap-up and homework

Close in two minutes:

1. Restate the promise kept: "You now hold your nine jobs with their real material, a good checklist for each one written before any run, a two column scoring sheet, two private jobs, and a decision page with three lists and a retest date."
2. Homework (say it exactly): "This week, run your nine jobs on the one agent you use now, even if you test no other. Score correctness from the checklist with a scorer who did not do the work, write your taste words the same day, and move any job both the checklist and your judgment failed onto the list of jobs no agent gets yet. Bring one failed job, and the checklist line it failed, to your next session."
3. Final line: "Other people's grades describe other people's work. Test your agent on your own nine jobs, and let your own scoreboard make the next decision."

## Facilitator notes

- Keep all language plain. "Benchmark" may be used only with a concrete example, as it is given in this plan and on the handout: a set of real jobs, run the same way, scored from a written checklist.
- Do not present any product named in the news as approved or recommended. Claire Vo's full per-task scores are in her episode; this plan reports only the verified headline result, that Grok Bot came first by a lot, and teaches her method, not her ranking. The 53.9 percent and 10.8 percent figures are the published results of Zapier's AutomationBench as reported in Google's comparison and of Surge AI's DAYJOB: Tax, and should be described that way, with the pass rule named for the tax test.
- Treat every vendor number in the source stories as a claim the maker made about itself, and say so when you use one. The internal Android figures and early adopter figures in today's other stories are not used in this workshop for exactly that reason.
- Time discipline: protect the 25-minute build block. Cut Q&A before cutting building time.
