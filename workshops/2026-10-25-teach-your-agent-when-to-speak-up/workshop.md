## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you will write the one page that decides whether your AI helper gets used or gets forgotten: the morning brief it brings you, the short list of events that may interrupt your day, the list of things that must always wait, and the approvals it must stop and ask for before it sends, pays, signs, or deletes anything.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0-8 · The beautiful agent nobody opened + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "On October 21, 2026, Anthropic launched a personal agent called Claude Duet. It lives on your phone, brings you a morning brief, checks your accounts every few hours, keeps working while your laptop sleeps, and waits for your approval before it sends, pays, signs, or deletes anything. Claire Vo, who tests these tools for a living on her show How I AI, ran it through her real week: her inbox, her sales pipeline, a sales deck, a launch video, and basketball tickets for her kids."
- "Her verdict is the title of her episode: Claude Duet is beautiful. I still forgot to use it. The agent did the work quietly and never once came and got her, so it lost to everything else fighting for her attention. She did not reject it. She forgot it. Forgetting is how most AI helpers die in a small business. Not with an error. With silence."
- "Today you will write the page Duet was missing, for the helper you already have: when it speaks, when it waits, and when it must stop and ask."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **Work nobody sees might as well not happen.** An agent that prepares good work in a place you never look has not saved you time. It has added a place to feel guilty about.
2. **Interrupting is a budget, not a mood.** Your attention is the most expensive thing in your business. The agent may spend it only on events you named in advance, in writing, on a calm day.
3. **The dangerous verbs always wait.** Sending, paying, signing, and deleting stop for a person every time. Speed is for reading and drafting. Approval is for anything that leaves the building or cannot be undone.

"The brief. The knock list. The four gates."

### 8-18 · The four parts of a speaking-up page

Teach the four parts on the board. Use a home services business with nine staff as the running example.

**Part one: the morning brief.** "Name the one message your helper brings you at a set time each morning, and name what belongs in it. Five slots, no more: what came in overnight that needs you, today's money in and out, the one job that is stuck, the one person waiting on you, and what the helper finished while you slept. Five slots is a discipline. A brief with twenty items is a second inbox, and you already have one of those. Write the time the brief arrives and where it arrives: the screen you actually open first. A brief delivered to an app you open at noon is a noon brief, whatever the schedule says."

**Part two: the knock list.** "Name the two or three events that may interrupt your day. Not topics. Events, with a line drawn. A customer uses the word refund. A payment above an amount you set fails or lands. A job booked for tomorrow still has nobody assigned at noon today. Each knock must carry three things: what happened, why it cannot wait for the brief, and the one action it is asking you for. Then run the mute test on every item: if this knocked nine times tomorrow, would you mute the helper by lunch? If yes, the event is too broad or the line is too low. Fix it on paper today. In a real week, a helper that knocks too often gets muted, and a muted helper is a forgotten helper with extra steps."

**Part three: the never list.** "Name what never interrupts, no exceptions, and waits for the morning brief. Newsletters. Reports that are on schedule. Praise. Anything that is interesting but not urgent. Write the never list down, because in the moment everything feels urgent to a machine that cannot tell a fire from a firefly. The never list is also where you protect your evenings: outside the hours you name, only a knock list event may reach you, and most businesses find their knock list has no evening members at all."

**Part four: the four gates and the one wall.** "Copy the gates Anthropic shipped with Duet, because they are the right four. The helper stops and asks before it sends a message in your name, before it pays or charges anything, before it signs anything, and before it deletes anything. Drafting is free. Those four verbs cost approval, every time, no matter how well last week went. Then choose your one wall: the thing the helper may never do, even if you ask for it in a hurry at midnight. Anthropic's walls are trading shares and moving money between accounts. Yours might be promising a customer a date, or ordering stock, or replying to a complaint. One wall, written down, named by you."

Then the habit that keeps it alive:

**The opening rule.** "The page is a bargain. The helper knocks only by these rules. You open the brief every morning. If you stop opening, the helper is not failing. The bargain is. Every forgotten agent in the research for this workshop was forgotten from both sides: it never knocked, and nobody looked."

*Facilitator note:* name the trap out loud. "The failure is not an agent that works badly. The failure is a good agent in the wrong room, at the wrong volume. A desk worker who lives in shared documents needs the helper inside those documents, with the team. An owner who runs on a phone between jobs needs the brief and the knock. Match the room before you judge the helper."

### 18-28 · Watch it work

Work one example live. Use two printed weeks for the same home services business. Keep it to ten minutes.

1. "Here is the week without the page." *(The helper files everything neatly into a channel. The owner opens it twice. A refund request sits for two days. A payment failure is discovered by the bookkeeper on Friday. The owner says the tool is fine and stops paying for it in month two.)* "Nothing broke. That is the point. Forgetting has no error message."
2. "Here is the same week with the page." *(The brief arrives at 7:00 AM on the phone: overnight messages, today's money, one stuck job, one waiting customer, two finished drafts. The refund request knocks at 10:40 AM with the amount and the one action asked. The payment failure knocks at noon the day it happens.)*
3. "Here is the mute test catching a bad knock." *(The first draft of the knock list said: knock when a review arrives. The shop gets nine reviews in a busy week, eight of them kind. The list is rewritten: reviews wait for the brief, except a review of two stars or fewer, which knocks with the text attached.)* "The knock survived because the line moved, on paper, before the owner muted the whole helper over kind reviews."
4. "Here is a gate doing its job." *(The helper drafts a reply to an unhappy customer and a refund of 180 dollars. The send waits. The payment waits. The owner reads both with a thumb, changes one sentence, approves.)* "Ninety seconds of owner time. The alternative was an unapproved refund and a message in the owner's name that the owner never saw."
5. "Here is the wall doing its job." *(At 9:30 PM the owner, tired, tells the helper to promise the customer a Saturday visit. The page says promising dates is the wall. The helper drafts the promise and holds it for the morning brief instead.)* "The wall is not the helper being difficult. The wall is the owner, on a calm day, protecting the owner on a tired night."

*Facilitator note:* if the room says their helper cannot do some of this yet, answer with the shape point: "Write the page for the helper you want, then set up the parts your current tool supports, and keep the page. The page outlives the tool. When you change tools next year, the new one gets this page on day one instead of starting from silence."

### 28-33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you will get, and the answers:

- "Will my customers know an agent wrote the draft?": "They will know what you send them. That is why sends sit behind your approval. The helper drafts at machine speed. You send at human responsibility. Nothing in this page lets a message leave in your name that you have not seen."
- "What if the brief is wrong one morning?": "Then you correct one slot, in the brief, and the correction becomes a standing instruction. A brief you read is a brief that improves. A brief nobody opens can be wrong for a month and nobody knows, which is the failure this workshop exists to prevent."
- "What does it cost me?": "One hour today, five minutes each morning to open the brief, and the approvals you were always going to owe on sends and payments. The page itself is free. The expensive version is the one most businesses are running now: a subscription for a helper nobody opens."

### 33-58 · Build your speaking-up page

Everyone works solo or in pairs on their speaking-up page (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "Read me your five brief slots. Which one would you miss most if it vanished?"
- "Read your knock list out loud. Run the mute test: nine knocks tomorrow, are you still glad by lunch?"
- "What is on your never list? Say one thing that feels urgent but belongs there."
- "Read the four gates back to me. Which of the four has your business been letting a tool do unapproved?"
- "What is your one wall? Say it as a full sentence."
- "When does the brief arrive, and on which screen, the one you truly open first?"

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their knock list and their one wall in thirty seconds. "My helper may knock when ___. It may never ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**The failure gallery.** "The agent that prepared a beautiful brief in a channel its owner opened twice in a month, then got cancelled as useless. The helper that knocked eleven times on its first day and was muted by dinner, refund request and all. The owner who let a tool send price quotes unapproved for one busy week and spent the next week apologizing for a number nobody chose. The evening promise made by a tired owner through a willing agent, which the whole team then had to keep. Every one of these is prevented by a sentence on the page you just wrote. None of them is prevented by a better model next quarter."

### 58-60 · Wrap-up and homework

Close in two minutes:

1. Restate the promise kept: "You now hold a one-page set of rules that says what your helper brings you each morning, what may interrupt your day, what must always wait, and what it may never do without you."
2. Homework (say it exactly): "This week, set up the morning brief with your five slots and turn on only your knock list. Count the knocks for five days. Bring the count, and the one knock you wish you had not received, to your next session."
3. Final line: "A helper that never speaks up gets forgotten. A helper that speaks up under your rules gets promoted. Write the rules while the choice is still yours."

## Facilitator notes

- Keep all language plain. "Approval gate" may be used only with its one-sentence explanation: a step where the helper stops and waits for a named person to say yes before it acts.
- Do not present any product named in the news as approved or recommended. The speaking-up page is the deliverable; the products are the examples.
- Treat every vendor number in the source story as a claim the vendor made about itself, and say so when you use one. The reviewer's findings are her reported experience from one week of testing, and should be described that way.
- Time discipline: protect the 25-minute build block. Cut Q&A before cutting building time.
