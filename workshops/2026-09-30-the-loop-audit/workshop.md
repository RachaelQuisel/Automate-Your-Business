# The Loop Audit: Stop Automating Tasks, Start Automating Loops

**Source:** Lenny's Podcast: Product | Career | Growth — Why companies are becoming a series of loops | Anish Acharya (a16z)

**Audience:** Small business owners and operators

---

## 1. The promise

Most people automate one task at a time and end up with twelve disconnected tools and the same
amount of work. By the end of this hour you will have mapped one piece of recurring work in your
business as a complete loop — trigger, steps, decision, output, and the point where it hands off
to a human — and you will know which single step to build first.

You are not going to build the whole loop today. You are going to know exactly what it is.

## 2. What they leave with

- One filled Loop Audit worksheet for a real piece of their own work.
- The five parts of that loop, written down in their own words.
- The one step they will build this week, named and sized.
- A written escape hatch — what the loop does when it gets stuck.

## 4. The teaching

### Idea 1 — The unit of automation is a loop, not a task

**The idea.** Automating a single task saves minutes. Automating a whole loop — where finishing
one step starts the next — changes how long the work takes end to end.

**Where it comes from.** [snip: Software Work Is Becoming Interconnected Loops](https://share.snipd.com/snip/5f2b7be5-5231-441c-8e34-e894744886a9)
and [snip: Companies Will Become Cascading AI Loops](https://share.snipd.com/snip/ed696730-9c5c-4df4-8453-68b10151ba63).

**What it means for a 10-person business.** The podcast example is software: a bug report comes
in, and five minutes later the bug has been reproduced, fixed, reviewed, deployed, and the
customer has been emailed. No human touched it. Translate that out of engineering and it is any
piece of work in your business that always follows the same shape.

A new client signs. Today that means: someone notices the signed contract, someone creates the
folder, someone sends the welcome email, someone builds the intake form, someone books the kickoff
call, someone adds them to the invoicing list. Six people-touches, spread over three days, and
each one waits on someone remembering.

As a loop, it is one trigger and six steps. The contract getting signed starts all of it.

**The example to use.** Walk the room through the signed-contract loop above out loud, naming who
does each step in a typical small business. Then ask: how long between the signature and the
kickoff call being on the calendar? The answer is always days. The work itself is eleven minutes.

**The common objection.** *"My work isn't repetitive enough for that."*

The honest answer: some of it isn't, and that part stays with you. But the test is not whether the
work is interesting. The test is whether it follows the same shape every time. Invoicing follows
the same shape. Onboarding follows the same shape. Chasing a late payment follows the same shape.
Deciding what to charge does not. You are looking for shape, not simplicity.

---

### Idea 2 — Every working loop has five parts, and the fifth is the one people skip

**The idea.** A loop that runs without you has a trigger, steps, a decision, an output, and an
escape hatch. Leave out the escape hatch and the loop will eventually do something wrong quietly.

**Where it comes from.** [snip: Kavak Agents Learn From Human Rescue Calls](https://share.snipd.com/snip/5fb51c64-aeec-40d9-b97b-cd6fad6f186d)
and [snip: Automate Growth Experiments Until They Plateau](https://share.snipd.com/snip/9bb2fc5f-11f6-406c-b098-baa9a346c332).

**What it means for a 10-person business.** Write these five on the whiteboard and keep them up
for the rest of the hour:

1. **Trigger** — the one thing that starts the loop. A form submitted. A payment cleared. A date
   arriving. If you cannot name the trigger in one sentence, you have two loops, not one.
2. **Steps** — what happens, in order, every time. No "sometimes we also".
3. **Decision** — the fork in the middle. Usually one question with two answers: is this a new
   client or a returning one, is this invoice over $5,000 or under.
4. **Output** — the thing that exists when the loop finishes. A sent email. A filled record. A
   booked call. If nothing exists at the end, the loop did not finish.
5. **Escape hatch** — what happens when the loop gets stuck, and who it calls.

**The example.** Kavak sells used cars. Their automated customer agents handle the conversation,
and when one gets stuck it calls a human. Two things happen on that call: the customer gets
helped, and the automation gets a record of what it got wrong, so it does not get stuck the same
way twice. The escape hatch is not a failure. It is how the loop gets better.

The other half of this comes from the growth-experiment snip: the loop runs the repetitive part —
generate, measure, ship the winner — and the human comes back in when improvement flattens out.
The loop handles the hill. The human picks which hill.

**The common objection.** *"If it needs a human anyway, what did I automate?"*

The answer: the 90% of cases that are ordinary. Before, a person handled 100 invoices to catch the
three that were wrong. After, a person handles three. You did not remove the human. You moved them
to the exceptions, which is the only part that needed judgment.

### Idea 3 — Start by handing your existing people the tools, not by redesigning the company

**The idea.** The first move is putting automation tools in front of the people already doing the
work. Redesigning roles and org charts comes later, if at all.

**Where it comes from.** [snip: AI Will Redesign Companies Before Replacing Jobs](https://share.snipd.com/snip/555aad70-0e51-4d20-922e-0b1f502ab48a),
[snip: AI Adoption Starts Before Company Reorganization](https://share.snipd.com/snip/00dd4c11-dbca-4c11-8a9d-c3573f060bbd),
and [snip: Ship One AI Project Every Week](https://share.snipd.com/snip/cbf58bea-7a9f-4e56-9f9d-d98ba399b1fb).

**What it means for a 10-person business.** Two facts from the podcast, both worth saying out loud
because they cut against what people expect:

- Kavak trained *mechanics* — not engineers — for six weeks, and each one finished by shipping a
  working production agent.
- The teams reported to have gained the most compressed two years of planned work into three
  months. They did not cut staff. They did more.

The person in your business who knows the most about your invoicing loop is the person who does
your invoicing. They are also the person most likely to want it automated, because they are the
one doing it 40 times a month. The common fear that your team will resist this is mostly not
borne out — they want the leverage.

**The example.** The one-project-a-week habit. Build something small every week, and it is allowed
to be useless. The podcast's example is a personalized Mother's Day slideshow made from old
messages and photos. The point is not the slideshow. The point is that you cannot develop a feel
for what these tools can and cannot do by reading about them, and a low-stakes build is the
cheapest way to find the edges.

**The common objection.** *"My team is already stretched. I can't add a training project."*

The honest answer: this is real, and it is the reason to start with one loop rather than a program.
One person, one loop, two hours a week. If that does not pay for itself within a month, stop.

## 5. Live build — the signed-contract loop, 10 minutes

**What Rachael builds on screen:** the first two steps of the new-client loop.

- **Tool:** Airtable (base + one automation). Any form-plus-database tool works the same way.
- **Trigger:** a record in `Clients` changes status to `Signed`.
- **Step 1:** create a folder structure for that client.
- **Step 2:** send the welcome email, with the client's name and kickoff link filled in.
- **Visible result:** change the status on screen, and the room watches the email land in an inbox
  and the folder appear.

**Say while building:** "I am not building the whole loop. I am building the trigger and the first
two steps. The other four steps get added one per week."

**Narrate the five parts as they appear.** Point at the screen and name each one — trigger, step,
step. Then ask the room where the decision and the escape hatch would go. Do not tell them; make
them place it. The answer for the escape hatch: if the welcome email fails to send, the loop
should put a task on a real person's list, not fail silently.

**If the demo breaks:** have a pre-built version of the same automation in a second browser tab
with a run history already showing. Switch to it, say "here's one I built earlier so you can see
the run log", and keep going. Do not debug live — it costs four minutes and teaches nothing.

## 6. The exercise — The Loop Audit, 14 minutes

Handout page 1 is the five parts. Page 2 is the audit grid.

**The exact instruction to say out loud:**

> "Take four minutes and write down every piece of work in your business that happens more than
> once a week and follows the same shape every time. Do not judge them. Just list them. Then
> circle the one that annoys you most. That is the one you are mapping for the next ten minutes."

Then, at the 4-minute mark:

> "Now fill in the five parts for that one. Trigger, steps, decision, output, escape hatch. If you
> get stuck on steps, write them as if you were telling a new hire what to do on their first day."

**Two examples for anyone who freezes:**

1. *Late invoice chase.* Trigger: an invoice passes 30 days unpaid. Steps: pull the invoice, send
   reminder one, wait seven days, send reminder two with the original attached, wait seven days,
   flag for a phone call. Decision: is the balance over $5,000? If yes, skip straight to the phone
   call. Output: either the invoice is marked paid, or a call is on someone's calendar. Escape
   hatch: the client replies with a question, so the loop stops and hands the whole thread to a
   person.
2. *New lead from the website.* Trigger: the contact form is submitted. Steps: create the lead
   record, send the acknowledgment, pull the company's website and add a one-paragraph summary to
   the record, assign an owner. Decision: is this inside our service area? If no, send the polite
   decline. Output: a lead record with an owner and a next action. Escape hatch: the form has no
   phone number and no company name, so it goes to a person to look at.

**While circulating, watch for the two failure patterns:**

- Someone has written a trigger that is actually a person remembering. "When I notice that..." is
  not a trigger. Push them to the event underneath it.
- Someone has written fifteen steps. That is three loops. Have them cut it at the first natural
  finish line.

## 7. Share-out and where this breaks — 8 minutes

**Three questions to ask the room:**

1. "Who has a trigger that turned out to be a person remembering something?" (Almost every hand.
   This is the most common finding and it normalizes it.)
2. "Read me your escape hatch." (Take three. Most will be weak on the first pass. Improve one live.)
3. "What is the first step you are building this week?" (Go around fast, one sentence each.)

**Where this breaks — say all four of these plainly:**

- **A loop built on a messy process automates the mess.** If two people do the same job two
  different ways, pick one way first, on paper, before you build anything.
- **A silent loop is worse than no loop.** If a step fails and nobody finds out, you will discover
  it through an angry client. Every loop needs a visible failure — a task, an alert, something a
  person sees.
- **Loops drift.** The welcome email you automated in March is out of date by September and nobody
  has read it since. Put a calendar reminder to reread every automated message twice a year.
- **The fifth step is where people quit.** Steps one and two are fun. Step five is a tedious
  integration with your accounting software. Build in the order the work flows, not in the order of
  what is enjoyable, or you will end up with four beginnings and no endings.

## 8. Close and the one-week assignment

**The close:**

> "You did not come here to buy software. The loop you just mapped is the actual asset — it is the
> first time that work has existed as one thing instead of six things you remember. The tools change
> every six months. The map doesn't."

**The assignment — one week, one line to write now:**

> "On the bottom of your handout, write one sentence: 'This week I will build the [trigger] for my
> [loop name] loop.' Just the trigger. Not the steps. When something in your business can start
> itself, you will have proven the whole thing works, and the steps are the easy part."

## 9. Facilitator notes

**Prepare beforehand:**
- Airtable base with a `Clients` table, a `Signed` status, and the working automation.
- A second browser tab with the same automation already built and a populated run history, for the
  demo-breaks fallback.
- The five parts written on the whiteboard before anyone walks in, so it is up the whole hour.
- Printed handouts. People map better on paper than on a laptop, and laptops open means email open.

**Timing risks:**
- Idea 3 is the segment to cut if running long. It is the least hands-on and the assignment carries
  its point. Cutting it buys 10 minutes.
- The live build wants to run to 15 minutes. Set a visible timer. Two steps is the whole scope.
- Do not let the share-out become a discussion between two people. One sentence each, keep moving.

**Questions that will come up:**

- *"Which tool should I use?"* — Whatever already holds your data. If your client list is in
  Airtable, build it in Airtable. If it is in a spreadsheet, the honest first step is getting it
  out of the spreadsheet. Do not switch tools to start; switching tools is how people spend three
  months not automating anything.
- *"How much does this cost?"* — The first loop, at this size, is usually under $50 a month in
  tooling. Say that, then say the real cost is the two hours a week to build it.
- *"Is this going to replace my assistant?"* — No, and say it directly. The loop takes the 90% that
  is ordinary. Your assistant gets the exceptions, which is the part that needed a person anyway.
  If someone in the room is hoping the answer is yes, the Kavak example is the useful one: the human
  on the rescue call is the reason the automation keeps improving.
- *"What if I get the loop wrong?"* — You will. Build the trigger and one step, watch it run ten
  times, then add the next step. Ten runs tells you more than any amount of planning.
