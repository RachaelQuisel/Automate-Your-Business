# Make Your AI Helper Interview You Before It Starts

**Title:** Make Your AI Helper Interview You Before It Starts
**Source:** Soft Fork snips, 2026-10-31: Snip 1 (make Claude interview you before multi-step work, Ruben Hassid via The Neuron, 2026-10-31), Snip 2 (Gemini creates, previews, and edits files inside the chat, Google, 2026-10-29), Snip 10 (Claude Duet shows a planned action and waits for approval before sending, paying, signing, or deleting, Digi Aura week of October 31, 2026), Snip 7 (Kin: Allow, Ask, or Block rules for each action, Daily AI for Work Pulse, 2026-10-31)

**Length:** 60 minutes
**Audience:** Small business owners and operators: no technical background assumed

## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you will write a short about-me file that holds the facts your helper should never have to ask about twice, collect one or two examples of your best finished work, and build your own opening script for big jobs: state the task, state what success looks like, require every question in one batch before anything is drafted, then require a short plan and a wait for your approval. You will leave with the file started, the opening script written in your own words, and one real job already run through it, with the plan fixed while fixing was still free.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0-8 · The intern who asks, the wrong draft, and the folder it reads + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "This week, The Neuron picked a Skill of the Day from Ruben Hassid's newsletter. It is one line he adds at the start of every big request in Claude Cowork, the desktop app that works on files in a folder you choose. The line tells the helper to ask its questions before it answers, to gather enough context first. Instead of guessing, the helper shows a short form with buttons and rankings. He answers in a couple of minutes. The helper shows a plan. He adjusts it. Only then does it build."
- "Everyone in this room knows the other version. You ask for a document. The helper disappears for four minutes and returns with something beautiful, confident, and wrong: wrong customer, wrong tone, wrong promise. Then you spend forty minutes editing the wrong thing, because starting over feels rude. The interview costs two minutes at the front and deletes that whole afternoon."
- "The same pause is appearing everywhere this month. Anthropic's Duet preview shows you the planned action and waits for your approval before it sends a message, makes a payment, signs a document, or deletes anything. A new tool called Kin gives every action a rule: Allow, Ask, or Block. The products differ. The pause is the same, and the pause is the product."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **Standing facts live in a file.** Your role, your customers, your prices and times, the things you never offer, and how you sound. Written once, in a short about-me file, with examples of your best work beside it. Never retyped into every request, and never left for the helper to guess.
2. **One-time facts come from the interview.** What this job is for, who reads it, what good looks like this time, and what must not happen. The helper asks for all of it in one batch, before it writes a word, because a wrong answer at the start costs one click and a wrong draft costs the day.
3. **The plan is approved before the work exists.** A short plan, shown and fixed while everything is still words. Moving a step in a plan is free. Moving a step in a finished document is an afternoon. Silence is never an approval, and the helper waits.

"File first. Questions in one batch. Plan approved. Then the draft."

### 8-18 · The six steps

Teach the six steps on the board. Use a home services and rental care business as the running example. The business cleans houses, turns over a small number of rentals between guests, and answers guest messages every day. Its example job is the customer reply its helper used to get wrong.

**Step one: build the standing folder, and keep it small.** "One short about-me file: who you are, who you serve, the standing facts a reply must never contradict (check-in time, parking, the services you do not offer), and how you sound in one paragraph. Beside it, one or two examples of finished work you are proud of, because one real example teaches tone better than ten adjectives. Keep the folder small on purpose. A helper that can read and write the folder you share should be shared a folder with only what this work needs in it. The source post carries that caution plainly: the app is a research preview, it can misread files, and it can touch whatever folder you give it, so give it a small one."

**Step two: write your opening script, once, in your own words.** "Four parts, in this order. The task, in one sentence. What success looks like, in one sentence. The instruction: ask me every question you need to do this well, in one batch, before you write anything. And the finish: then show me a short plan and wait for my approval before drafting. Write it on paper first. If any part needs a technical word, rewrite it until it does not."

**Step three: answer the questions in one batch, and read them as a mirror.** "The form is not a toll gate. It is a check on your own request. If the helper asks who this is for and you do not know, the request was not ready, and finding that out took ninety seconds instead of a finished draft. Answer honestly and completely. Every skipped question becomes a guess the helper makes alone, at full confidence, in your name."

**Step four: fix the plan, out loud.** "Read the plan the way you would read a new hire's plan on their first Friday. Is the order right. Is anything missing that a customer will notice. Is anything promised that you do not offer. Change it in words, not in your head, so the change is on the record. The plan you approve is the standard the finished work gets judged against, by you and by anyone you show it to later."

**Step five: approve, or send it back, in writing.** "One word is enough, approved, or one sentence about what to change. Never let a plan drift into work because nobody replied. That rule is the whole difference between Duet's design and a helper that acts while you are at lunch: the planned action is shown, and it waits. Your opening script builds the same wait into any chat tool, including the ones with no special feature for it, using the plain version from the source: state the task and success, ask for every question in one batch, require the plan, and require the wait."

**Step six: finish the file where it started, and graduate the repeated answers.** "When the draft arrives, fix it in the same conversation. This week Google made that literal for Gemini users: files can now be created, previewed, and edited inside the chat, by typing in full screen, by highlighting a passage and asking about it, or by asking the helper to refine it, with PDFs working on phones and the other formats on the web for now. However you edit, end with one habit: when a question has now been asked and answered three times, that answer graduates. Move it into the about-me file, or into a written procedure for that job, so the interview gets shorter every month and your attention is saved for what is actually new."

**The Allow, Ask, or Block rule (from Snip 7).** "Before the first real job, sort your helper's possible actions into three piles. Allow: reading the folder, drafting, summarizing. Ask: anything that leaves the business, a message, a booking, a change a customer will see. Block: payments, deletions, and sharing private information, until you say otherwise in writing. Kin sells this as a product this week. You can run it as a paragraph in your about-me file today."

### 18-28 · Watch it work

Work one example live. Use the same home services and rental care business. Keep it to ten minutes.

1. "Here is the old way." *(The owner pastes a guest message into a chat and types: draft a reply. The draft comes back warm, fluent, and wrong. It offers an early arrival the business never promises, because cleaning takes the full time between guests. It mentions a parking arrangement the property does not have. The owner rewrites it herself, at night, and wonders why the help needs this much help.)* "Nothing in that draft was stupid. It was uninformed, which is worse, because it sounds sure."
2. "Here is the standing folder." *(One page. The business, the street it serves, check-in and check-out times, where guests park, the short list of things never offered, one paragraph on tone: warm, direct, short sentences. Beside it, two replies the owner is proud of.)* "Twenty minutes to write, once. This page is now the most valuable file in the business that nobody can see from the street."
3. "Here is the interview." *(The owner uses her opening script. The form asks three questions. Is the date in the message the arrival date or the event date. Has this guest already received the standard arrival information once. Does anything in the recent notes change what we should say about parking this week. She answers in under two minutes.)* "Read those questions again. Every one of them is a draft she used to fix by hand. The helper did not get smarter. It got briefed."
4. "Here is the plan, and the fix." *(Four lines: confirm the dates, answer the parking question using the standing fact, name the one thing that changed this week, close with the check-in time. The owner changes one thing: move the changed item before the parking answer, because it is the reason for the message. Approved, in writing, in the thread.)* "One change, made while the work was still four lines long. That change used to cost a full rewrite."
5. "Here is the draft." *(Accurate on every standing fact, warm, short, no promise the business does not keep. The owner edits two words and sends it herself.)* "Two words. Compare the night she used to spend. The two minutes of questions bought back the rewrite, and the record of what good meant, the folder, the questions, the approved plan, is still sitting in the thread for the next person who has to answer the same guest."

*Facilitator note:* if the room says their tool has no question form, answer with the plain version: "The form is a convenience, not the point. The Neuron prints a version that works in any chat: state the task and success, ask for every question in one batch, require a short plan, and require a wait for your approval. Buttons are nice. The order is the product."

### 28-33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you will get, and the answers:

- "Is this not just a longer prompt with extra steps?": "A prompt is you guessing what the helper needs to know. An interview is the helper telling you what it needs to know. The lists are different, and yours will always miss the thing that felt too obvious to write down. The extra steps are two minutes. The step they replace is the rewrite."
- "What if I just click through the questions to get to the draft?": "Then the interview becomes theater, the way approving without reading did in yesterday's lesson. The guard is one instruction: only ask when my answer changes the result. A short form you actually read beats a long form you perform."
- "What belongs in the about-me file, and what must never go in it?": "Standing facts and tone, yes. Passwords, card numbers, private customer details, and anything you would not hand a new hire on day one, no. The folder is shared with a tool that can misread files. Small, current, and boring is the standard. Review it monthly, the way you would review what a team member believes about the business."

### 33-58 · Write your own about-me file and opening script, and run one real job

Everyone works solo or in pairs on their own next real job (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "Read me your about-me file. Which fact in it would a wrong draft most often get wrong?"
- "Show me one example of your best work. What does it teach about your tone that no adjective could?"
- "Read your opening script out loud. Does it state success in one sentence a customer would recognize?"
- "Which questions did the helper ask? Which one surprised you, and what does that say about your original request?"
- "Is there a question it should have asked and did not? Add the standing fact behind it to your file, now."
- "Read the plan. Where is the one change you made, and why?"
- "Sort this job's actions: what may the helper do without asking next time, what must it ask about, and what is blocked? Say them out loud."
- "Which answer from today graduates into your file after three repeats? Mark it."
- "Where will this finished file be fixed: in the same chat, or somewhere else? Name the place before you start."

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their task, the best question the helper asked, and the one change they made to its plan, in thirty seconds. "My task was ___. The best question it asked was ___. The change I made to its plan was ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**The failure gallery.** "The draft that promised what the business never offers, sent before anyone checked. The helper pointed at the owner's whole drive on day one, because the folder step felt like paperwork. The interview answered by clicking, twelve questions, zero read. The plan that drifted into a sent message because nobody wrote the word approved or the word wait. The standing file from last year, with last year's prices, quoted with total confidence today. The example pasted in from a competitor, which taught the helper someone else's voice perfectly. Every one of these is prevented by a rule you used today: a small current folder, questions in one batch and actually answered, a plan fixed in words, approval in writing, and repeated answers graduated into the file."

### 58-60 · Wrap-up and homework

Close in two minutes:

1. Restate the promise kept: "You now hold a standing about-me file, one or two examples of your best work, an opening script in your own words, and one real job run through the full order: questions, plan, approval, draft, and the fix made where the work started."
2. Homework (say it exactly): "This week, use the opening script for every big job, all week, no exceptions for jobs that feel too small to interview for. Each time the same question appears for the third time, graduate its answer into your about-me file. Bring your script, your updated file, and the before-and-after of one job, the old draft and the interviewed draft, to your next session."
3. Final line: "Make your AI helper interview you before it starts. The file remembers, the questions aim, and your approval is the gate the work walks through, every time."

## Facilitator notes

- Keep all language plain. "Interview" here means a short set of questions the helper asks before drafting, in one batch, plus a short plan that waits for approval. No product feature is required; the plain version works in any chat.
- Do not present any product named in the news as approved or recommended. Cowork is described in the source as a research preview that can misread files, and the plan teaches the small-folder caution from that sourcing. Duet's approval step and Kin's Allow, Ask, or Block rules are described as their makers and the roundups present them. Gemini's in-chat editing availability is stated with its platform split (PDFs on web and mobile, other formats on the web for now) exactly as Google announced it on October 29, 2026.
- Treat the approved plan as the record of what good meant for that job. Keep it with the finished work; it is what makes the next interview shorter and the next review faster.
- Time discipline: protect the 25-minute build block. Cut Q&A before cutting building time.
