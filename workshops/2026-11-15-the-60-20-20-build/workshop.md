## 1. The promise

By 3:30 you'll be able to split any one job you want AI to help with into three honest piles —
ready now, almost ready, and just a guess — instead of one big yes-or-no decision about whether
"AI can do this." By 4:00 you'll have sorted one real job from your business into those three
piles, and built the piece that's actually ready.

## 2. What they leave with

- One job from their business split into three written piles: automate fully, draft-and-check,
  keep a person on it.
- A built (or started) automation for the "ready now" pile only — nothing else.
- A written line for what moves a piece from one pile up to the next.
- A filled handout showing exactly which part of their job landed in which pile, and why.

## 4. The concept

**The idea.** Stop asking "can AI do this job" as one yes-or-no question. Split the job into
three honest piles instead: about 60% of it is stuff AI already does reliably — let it run.
About 20% is close but not solid — let it draft, and a person checks before anything goes out.
The last 20% is just a hopeful guess right now — keep a person doing it fully, and don't force AI
into it before it's ready. Build each pile as its own separate piece, so when a piece gets more
reliable, you move it up a pile without rebuilding the whole thing.

**What it means for a 10-person business.** The podcast's example is a product team deciding what
to ship: roughly 60% of a product's functionality should be things the model handles today,
without drama; 20% should be capabilities that are nearly there, built so they can slot in as
they mature; and 20% is reserved for the promising-but-unproven glimpses — kept loose, not load-
bearing, so the product doesn't get rebuilt from scratch every time a new model ships. The reason
you can't just automate everything at once: frontier models are, in Tulsee Doshi's words, like
Snape's potions class — the people who build them control the ingredients, but real surprises
still show up downstream. You don't get to assume a model will behave the same way on your
weirdest 20% of cases as it did on your first ten test runs.

Translate that out of product management and into any small business: pick one job you want AI
to help with — not your whole operation, one job — and sort the pieces of it into the same three
piles. The piece that's dead simple and consistent gets automated outright. The piece that's
close gets a draft, checked by a person before it counts as done. The piece that's genuinely a
judgment call, where getting it wrong is expensive or embarrassing, stays fully human — for now.
That last part matters: "not yet" is not the same as "never." The pile a thing sits in today is
not where it has to sit in six months.

**The example to use.** A ten-person landscaping company gets quote requests by email and web
form all day: a name, an address, a job description ("backyard's a mess, need it cleared and
mulched"), sometimes a photo. Today, the owner reads every single one by hand before anyone can
quote it.

Split that one job into three piles:

- **Ready now (60%):** reading the incoming message and pulling out the structured facts —
  name, address, job type, size if it's mentioned, whether a photo was attached. AI is reliably
  good at this kind of extraction. Let it fill the lead record every time, with no person
  checking the routine ones.
- **Almost ready (20%):** drafting a price estimate from the owner's own price sheet and similar
  past jobs. AI can produce a plausible number, but pricing has judgment baked into it — slope,
  access for equipment, how overgrown the yard actually is, whether this is a client worth a
  discount. A person checks the draft before it goes out. Nothing with a dollar figure leaves the
  building unseen.
- **Just a guess (20%):** deciding whether to take a job at all when it's the kind that bites you
  later — a tree limb near a power line, a drainage complaint from a difficult past client, a job
  that smells like a liability problem before the crew even shows up. This stays fully with the
  owner. It is not worth trying to automate yet, and a wrong guess here is the expensive kind of
  wrong.

**The receipts.**

- In 2024, Klarna said its AI customer-service assistant was doing the work of 700 full-time
  agents and handling two-thirds of all chats. By May 2025, CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski admitted
  the push toward full AI automation "went too far" and produced lower-quality service even
  though it was cheaper — and the company started hiring human agents back. Klarna had moved
  customer service's hardest judgment calls into the "automate fully" pile instead of the
  "draft, then a person checks" pile, and had to walk it back in public.
  [Entrepreneur: Klarna Is Hiring Customer Service Agents After AI Couldn't Cut It on Calls](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/klarna-ceo-reverses-course-by-hiring-more-humans-not-ai/)
- McDonald's and IBM ran an AI order-taking trial at roughly 100 drive-thrus starting in 2021,
  aiming for 95%-plus accuracy before any wider rollout. It never got there — a viral clip showed
  the system adding more than 20 extra boxes of chicken nuggets to one order — and McDonald's
  shut the whole test down in June 2024. Order-taking at a drive-thru, under time pressure, with
  no human checking before the order is confirmed, was a 20%-pile job that got put straight into
  the 60% pile.
  [Fortune: McDonald's AI order-taking drive-thru test ends](https://fortune.com/2024/06/17/mcdonalds-ai-order-taking-drive-thru-ends/)

**The common objection.** *"If I'm only automating the easy 60%, what did I actually gain?"*

The honest answer: you gained the part that was eating the most hours with the least thinking —
and you avoided the thing that actually kills most automation projects, which isn't automating
too little, it's refusing to ship anything until the hardest 20% works perfectly. Most owners who
try to automate everything at once end up with nothing live six months later. The landscaping
owner above gets every lead logged accurately, every single day, starting this week. The pricing
judgment and the liability calls stay exactly where they were. That's still a real win.

**FAQ**

- *"How do I know which pile something belongs in?"* Run it on ten real past examples from your
  own business and see how often you'd have to fix what it produced. Right nine or ten times out
  of ten with no fixing: ready now. Right about half the time, usually close but needs a look:
  almost ready. Wrong more often than not, or wrong in an expensive way: it's a guess, leave it
  with a person.
- *"What if I put something in the wrong pile at first?"* You will, especially at the start.
  That's exactly what the ten-example check is for — it's how a piece moves between piles, not a
  one-time verdict that locks it in forever.
- *"Doesn't keeping a person on the last 20% waste the whole point of automating?"* It's cheaper
  than the alternative: forcing AI into judgment calls it keeps getting wrong, then paying someone
  to clean up after it anyway — which is what both receipts above show happening in public.
- *"How often should I re-check the piles?"* Pick a date — a calendar reminder every quarter is
  enough — and re-run the ten-example check on whatever's sitting in "almost ready" and "just a
  guess." Models change faster than most businesses revisit old decisions.
- *"Does this apply to my whole business at once?"* No — one job at a time. Each job you hand to
  AI gets its own 60-20-20 split. Trying to sort your entire operation in one sitting is how this
  turns into a spreadsheet nobody finishes.

## 5. Live build — the "ready now" pile for the quote-request job, 25–30 minutes

**What Rachael builds on screen**, scoped to fill the whole open-build block — only the "ready
now" piece, nothing from the other two piles:

- **Tool:** Airtable (a form or an email-triggered automation feeding a table). Any tool that can
  read incoming text and write to a list works the same way.
- **Trigger:** a new quote-request email or web form submission arrives.
- **Step 1:** pull the structured fields out of the message — name, address, job type, size if
  mentioned, whether a photo was attached — and write them into a new row in the `Leads` table.
- **Step 2:** set that row's status to `Needs quote draft` — the handoff point into the "almost
  ready" pile, which a person does by hand today.
- **The cap (stop condition):** if a required field — address or job type — can't be found in the
  message, the automation does not guess at it. It sets the status to `Needs manual read` and
  stops there, instead of inventing a plausible-looking address.
- **Visible result:** a new row appears in `Leads` with the fields filled in and a clear status,
  seconds after a test email is sent.

**If the demo breaks:** have a pre-built version with a populated `Leads` table already showing
real-looking rows in a second tab. Switch to it, say "here's this morning's actual intake," and
keep going.

## 6. Open build and questions

- **What the room is doing:** sorting one real job from their own business into the three piles
  on the handout, then building the "ready now" piece of it — the same shape as the demo: read
  something that arrives, pull out the facts, write them somewhere, flag what's missing instead
  of guessing.
- **What to circulate and watch for:** people try to build all three piles at once in the time
  available. Stop them and ask: "which one pile are you building today?" Also watch for the
  almost-ready pile quietly getting treated as ready-now — if a draft number or draft message
  would go out without anyone looking at it, that's the wrong pile.
- **Restart line if the room stalls:** "Say the sentence out loud: 'The part of this job I'd trust
  without checking is ___. The part I'd want to see first is ___. The part I wouldn't hand over at
  all yet is ___.' Whatever fills those three blanks is your split."
- **Two speeds:** a fast finisher writes the handoff line for the "almost ready" pile — exactly
  what the draft needs to include so a person can check it fast. Someone still stuck with five
  minutes left should stop building and just write the three-sentence split from the restart
  line — that alone is a usable starting point for next week.

## 7. Close and the one-week assignment

**The close:** "You don't need AI that does the whole job. You need one honest list of which
third of the job it's actually ready for today — and the discipline to leave the other two-thirds
exactly where they are until they've earned their way up."

**The assignment:** "This week, write the three-pile split for one more job in your business —
just the list, on paper, no building required. Then run the ten-example check from the FAQ on
whatever you put in the 'almost ready' pile. If it's right eight or more times out of ten with no
fixing, it just earned its way into 'ready now.'"

## 8. Facilitator notes

**Prepare beforehand:**
- An Airtable base with a `Leads` table, a `Status` field, and the working intake automation.
- A second tab with a populated `Leads` table for the demo-breaks fallback.
- Printed handouts.

**Timing risks:**
- The receipts section tempts a longer debate about whether Klarna or McDonald's "should have
  known better." Cap it at the two stories as written — the point is the pile, not the company.
- The FAQ's ten-example check is the single most useful idea in the room after the build itself.
  If time is short, protect this over the second receipt.

**Questions beyond the FAQ that will come up:**
- *"What if a piece doesn't cleanly belong to one pile?"* Split the job smaller until it does. If
  you can't tell which pile something's in, it's usually two jobs pretending to be one.
- *"Who decides when something moves up a pile?"* The same person who owns the job today. This
  workshop doesn't hand that judgment to a tool — it just gives you a plain way to make the call
  and revisit it on a schedule instead of never.

## 9. Best practices nobody tells you

**Give the "ready now" pile a stop condition, not just a job.** Why it bites people: an automation
that's told to fill in a field will often fill it in with something plausible-looking rather than
admit it doesn't know — and a plausible-looking wrong address is worse than a blank one, because
nobody double-checks it. In this build: if the address or job type can't be found in the incoming
message, the automation sets the status to `Needs manual read` and stops, instead of guessing.

**Put a date on re-checking the other two piles — not "eventually."** Why it bites people: models
keep changing, but business owners rarely revisit a decision once it's made, so the "almost ready"
and "just a guess" piles quietly calcify into permanent human work long after the technology has
caught up. In this build: put a quarterly calendar reminder to re-run the ten-example check from
the FAQ on the quoting draft and the liability judgment call, and actually move something up a
pile when it earns it.
