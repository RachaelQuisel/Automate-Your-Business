## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you'll write a one-page job description for the one AI helper your business needs most: what it does, where it lives, what it may do alone, what needs your tap, what it never touches, and the plan it chose. You'll leave with a helper you could hand to a smart new hire, or to an agent, tomorrow morning.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0–8 · The week the wiring disappeared + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "This week the way you build an AI helper changed. A tool called n8n shipped something called n8n Agents. The pitch is one sentence: instead of drawing every step of a workflow, you describe the outcome in plain language, pick an AI model, connect your tools and channels, and the agent figures out the steps. Their demo is a Telegram assistant that turns photos of sticky notes into tasks. You photograph the sticky note on the wall, the agent creates the task. It logs receipts. It sorts email. The messiest inputs in a small business, and the agent eats them and produces structure."
- "The same idea showed up in video. HeyGen announced a shared timeline where an agent builds your marketing video and you point at a frame and leave a note, the way you would with a human freelancer. This shot, warmer. That cut, earlier. No re-prompting from scratch. Pointing and commenting."
- "And the third story is the warning label. An open-weights model called Kimi K2.6 ran agent swarms for five straight days of monitoring and incident response, up to three hundred sub-agents, and the model itself decided the orchestration. The plan walked. Nothing crashed. Every step was reasonable. The destination just drifted. Which is why the job description is now the most important document in your business, and why today you write one."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **Describe the job, don't draw the wiring.** The agent figures out the steps. Your work is the description: what the job is, where it lives, what good looks like.
2. **The plan is the thing you audit.** When the agent writes its own plan, you don't just check what it did. You check what it decided to do. A new line on yesterday's Authority Card.
3. **The prenup goes on the back.** The Pentagon ripped Claude out of its systems this week after a contract dispute. Removing an embedded agent is painful, said the analyst. So before you embed a helper deep, write the exit terms: how your data gets out, what the off-ramp is, whether the work could move.

"Describe it like a hire. Audit it like a plan. Prenup it like a marriage."

### 8–18 · The job description

"A good job description has four parts. That's the whole model. The sticky-note demo runs on all four, and yours will too."

Write the four parts on the board, with the n8n demo filling each one:

**Part 1: The job.** One paragraph. What it does, in plain words. The demo: "Turn photos of sticky notes into tasks, log receipts, sort email." Not "leverage AI-powered automation." A sentence you could say to a new hire on their first day. Small business version: "Read every new review, draft a reply in my voice, queue it for my tap." If you can't say it in one breath, the agent can't do it in one run.

**Part 2: Where it lives.** The channels. Where does the job arrive, and where does the work report back. The demo: photos arrive in Telegram, tasks land in the task list, receipts land in the expense folder. Small business version: reviews arrive from Google and Yelp, drafts land in a queue I check each morning. "If you don't name the channels, the helper will invent them, and it will invent wrong."

**Part 3: The boundaries.** Three columns, borrowed from yesterday's Authority Card: what it may do alone, what needs your tap, what it never touches. The demo's boundaries: it may read photos and create tasks alone; it needs your tap before sending anything to a customer; it never touches the bank account. "The never-touches column is still the most important thing you will write today."

**Part 4: The plan it chose.** The new line. When the agent figures out the steps, you review the steps. The demo's plan: read the photo, extract the text, guess the due date, create the task, file the receipt. "Ask the helper to show its plan before it runs the first time. Read the plan the way you'd read a new hire's first-day checklist. If the plan is wrong, the description was wrong. Fix the description."

"Notice what the four parts do. Part one says what. Part two says where. Part three says the limits. Part four says show your work. That is a complete management relationship on one page, whether the employee is a person or an agent."

*Facilitator note:* if anyone asks "isn't this just the Authority Card again," say yes, and that's the point. "Yesterday's card was the permission slip. Today's page is the job. Same discipline, bigger document. The card fits in a wallet. The job description runs the hire."

### 18–28 · Watch it work

Fill in one job description live for the review-response helper. No installation for attendees: follow-along demo, or narrate the sticky-note demo and map each part as you go. Keep it to ten minutes.

Work the example in plain words:

1. "Here's the job." *(Write: Job: read every new review on Google and Yelp, draft a reply in my voice, queue the draft for my morning tap. Never posts.)* "One paragraph. Say it in one breath."
2. "Here's where it lives." *(Write: Channels: reviews arrive from Google and Yelp. Drafts land in one queue I check each morning with coffee. Replies go out only from my phone.)* "Arrives here, reports there. Named, both ends."
3. "Here's the boundaries." *(Write: May do alone: read reviews, draft replies, flag the angry ones. Needs my tap: posting anything, promising a refund. Never touches: deleting a review, replying in a private channel.)* "Same three columns as yesterday's card. The discipline carries over."
4. "Here's the plan it chose." *(Write: Plan: check feeds at 7 AM, sort by urgency, draft in my voice from my three example replies, queue with the original review attached.)* "This is the new line. If the plan said 'check feeds at midnight and auto-post,' we'd catch it here, before it runs. That's the audit."

Then ask the room: "What would the plan look like for YOUR most annoying task? Not the perfect plan. The first-draft plan you'd want to review." That question teaches the whole workshop: the description is a draft you improve, and the review is the job.

*Facilitator note:* if it glitches, narrate the fix: "this is what managing one looks like." Ten minutes max, then open the floor.

### 28–33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you'll get, and the answers:

- "What if my description is bad and the agent does the wrong thing?": That's what part four is for. The plan review catches the bad description before it runs. A bad description with a plan review is a rough draft. A bad description without one is a liability. You will write bad first drafts. Everyone does. The review is the safety net.
- "I already have helpers with no job descriptions. What now?": Start with the riskiest one. Write its four parts tonight. You already wrote its Authority Card yesterday if you were here. Today's page is the card grown up: the same boundaries, plus the job and the plan. One page per helper. The inventory comes before everything.
- "Should the helper live in Telegram, or Slack, or email?": Wherever you already are. The n8n demo uses Telegram because the builder lives on their phone. Your helper should live where you will actually read its reports. The best channel is the one you check with coffee. Everything else is a graveyard.

### 33–58 · Write your own

Everyone works solo or in pairs on the job description (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "Which recurring headache do you want to hand off? Reviews, inbox, receipts, scheduling?"
- "Say the job in one breath. If it takes two breaths, it's two jobs."
- "Where does it arrive, and where does it report? Name both ends."
- "Now the boundaries. May do alone, needs your tap, never touches."
- "And the plan it chose. What steps would you expect to see? Write the first draft of the plan you'd want to review."

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their job description in thirty seconds. "My helper's job is ___. It lives in ___. It may ___, it needs my tap for ___, it never touches ___. The plan I'd review is ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**What could go wrong.** "This week we got two cautionary tales. First, the Pentagon ripped Claude out of its military systems after a contract dispute. An analyst said removing an embedded agent is painful, because once it's woven into the workflows, taking it out is surgery. That's the prenup lesson: write the exit terms before you embed. Second, the Kimi swarm that walked. Five days, no crash, every step reasonable, and the plan drifted to a different destination. That's the plan-review lesson: the longer the run, the further the walk. Your boundaries are the guardrail. Your plan review is the inspection. Your prenup is the divorce papers you hope to never use."

### 58–60 · Wrap-up and homework

"Describe the job, don't draw the wiring. The plan is the thing you audit. The prenup goes on the back."

Homework: write the one-page job description for the helper you need most. Show it to one person who knows your business and ask: "What did I leave out?" Run the plan review the first time it runs. Bring back three things: what the description got right, what the plan review caught, and one line you'd add to the prenup. If the first run was boring, the description was good. Boring is still the promotion criterion.

*Facilitator note:* hold up a filled job description as you say this. "Bring this page back filled in, or better, bring the helper it describes."

## 4. Plain-English word list

Keep this on your facilitator copy. If you're tempted to say the left column, say the right column instead, and only use the product's own word after the plain version.

| Instead of... | Say... |
|---|---|
| Agentic workflow / agent orchestration | The helper figures out the steps |
| Event-triggered agent | It runs when the thing happens |
| Confidence-based routing | It asks you when it's unsure |
| Model-determined orchestration | The agent writes its own plan |
| Plan drift / state degradation | The plan walked: each step was fine, the destination moved |
| Data residency / sovereignty | Your data stays in your country, on your rules |
| Vendor lock-in | Hard to leave: the removal would be painful |
| Off-ramp / exit terms | The prenup: how your data gets out |
| Execution-based pricing | You pay per job done, not per step taken |
| Shared artifact / collaboration surface | The page you both point at |
