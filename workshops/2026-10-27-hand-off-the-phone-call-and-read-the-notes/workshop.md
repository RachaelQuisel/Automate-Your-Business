## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you will learn how to hand off one real phone call from your business and still stay in charge of it. You will write a short brief before the call, decide in advance what the caller may agree to and what it must never agree to, and you will read the record after the call: the notes, the summary, and every promise the call made, turned into a dated task with a named owner. The call itself is the easy part. The brief and the record are the job.

Total: 25 minutes building, about 15 minutes Q&A, two minutes of close.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0-8 · The call you have been avoiding + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "On October 25, 2026, Elon Musk posted that Grok Bot, the always-on helper from xAI, can now call a person or a business for you, make a request on your behalf, take notes on the response, and send you a note summarizing the conversation. Coverage of the release underneath that post says the helper can sit on hold, work through press-a-number menus by pressing keys, and get transferred between departments without dropping the task, and that you can watch a live transcript while the call is happening."
- "Now forget the brand for a minute. Think of your own list. The supplier who only takes orders by phone. The utility hold queue. The warranty desk. The booking that needs confirming. The permit office. These are the calls nobody wants to make, and they are the calls that decide whether a job runs on time. They are also the calls where the outcome is usually one fact: a time, a date, a number, a yes, or a no. Facts fit in a summary. That is why a delegated call can work."
- "But here is the part the announcement does not cover, and it is the whole workshop. The announcement does not say which subscription plans include calling, which countries it works in, or what number shows up on the other person's phone. It also does not answer, in public, how the helper handles recording consent and disclosure, which vary by state and by country. So the careful move is not to hand an agent your phone list and hope. The careful move is to write the brief first, set the limits first, and read the record after. That is true whether a helper makes the call, a new hire makes it, or you make it yourself."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **The brief is the job. The call is just the brief being read out loud by someone patient.** If the brief is clear, any caller can do a good job. If the brief is vague, even a perfect caller will guess, and guesses on the phone become promises.
2. **The record is the product. If it is not written down, it did not happen.** A transcript shows what was actually said. A summary shows the outcome. Neither is useful until the promises in them become dated tasks with named owners.
3. **The limit is set in advance, in writing, before anyone dials.** What may be agreed to, what must never be agreed to, and how much may be spent are decided by you, before, not discovered after.

"Brief. Call. Read the record. File the outcome."

### 8-18 · The four steps, the brief, and the limits

Teach the four steps on the board. Use a home services and rental care business with nine staff as the running example. The business cleans houses, turns over a small number of rentals between guests, and sends people out for small repairs and checks.

**Step one: brief the call.** "Write it down before anyone dials. A brief has six parts, and the handout has a blank one for you to fill in. Who to call, with the number and the account or reference number if you have one. What to ask, in the order you want it asked, word for word where the words matter. What a good outcome looks like in one sentence. What the caller may agree to. What the caller must never agree to. And how the caller introduces itself and handles recording, so the call starts honestly."

**The may-agree list and the never-agree list.** "This is the heart of the workshop, so teach it slowly. The may-agree list is small and concrete: confirm a time that is already on the calendar, confirm a delivery window, ask for a reference number, ask for a name and a callback number, request that a document be sent to an email address you name. The never-agree list is the protection: never agree to a new price, a discount, a changed date that moves money, a contract change, a new service, or a payment, without coming back to you first. If the person on the other end offers a deal, the caller's job is to write it down exactly and bring it back, not to accept it on the spot. A caller who accepts a deal you never saw is not efficient. A caller who brings a deal back with the terms written down is doing the job."

**Step two: make the call.** "The caller dials, works through the menus, waits on hold, and handles a transfer without dropping the brief. The brief travels with the caller, which is why a transfer does not become a restart. On a transferred call, the caller re-states who they are calling for, what they are asking, and what has already been said, so the new person does not have to start from zero. If you are watching a live transcript, this is the part to watch: does the caller stay inside the brief, or does it start improvising? Improvising is the warning sign."

**Step three: read the record.** "When the call ends, read two things. First, the transcript, or the notes, for what was actually said, not what you hoped was said. Second, the summary, for the outcome. Then ask three questions. What facts did the call produce: names, times, dates, reference numbers? What promises were made, by either side? What is still unclear, and who will clear it up? Anything still unclear goes on the follow-up list, with a name and a date. It does not sit in the summary as a vague hope."

**Step four: file the outcome.** "The summary goes where the next person will find it, not in a chat thread that scrolls away and not in one person's inbox. Every promise from the call becomes a dated task with a named owner: the delivery on Thursday belongs to a person, the permit follow-up belongs to a person, the callback by Friday belongs to a person. If nobody owns it, the call did not finish. It just stopped ringing."

**The limits, and the key rule that travels with this workshop.** "Today's research carries a second guardrail, and it belongs here even though it sounds like a different topic. After a breach at the hosted side of an open source coding tool, where provider keys stored in a dashboard were used to run up charges on other people's accounts, one writer's rule was: a key that lives in a third-party dashboard should be able to lose you at most a number you chose in advance. One key per tool. A spending cap on the account, roughly twice your normal bill as a starting point. Spend alerts sent somewhere a named person actually reads. The parallel is exact. A delegated call is also spending authority, in promises instead of dollars. The cap for a call is the never-agree list. The alert is the summary you actually read. And just like a key, a call made in your name that you never saw is a liability, not a convenience. Set the limit before, read the record after."

**The review gate, borrowed from the tax story.** "Today's research also includes a tax automation that prepares returns end to end inside the software firms already use, and lands them review-ready, with every figure traced to its source document, for a person to review before filing. Same shape as the call. The automation does the work. The person reads the record and signs. Do not let a summary file itself. A person reads it, checks the facts that matter, and signs the tasks off. That signature is what turns a transcript into a decision."

### 18-28 · Watch it work

Work one example live. Use two printed weeks for the same home services and rental care business. Keep it to ten minutes.

1. "Here is the week without the brief." *(On Monday, a worker is told to call the supplier and sort out the delivery. He calls, waits twenty minutes, and the supplier offers a different product at a slightly higher price with delivery on Wednesday instead of Thursday. He says yes, because saying yes feels like finishing. On Thursday, the crew arrives and the product is wrong, the price is wrong, and the rental turnover it was needed for has already started. On Friday, the office calls the permit office about a different job and is told the reference number from Monday's call cannot be found, because nobody wrote it down. Two calls, two messes, and nobody did anything wrong except work without a brief.)* "Both calls took time. Neither call left a record anyone could check."
2. "Here is the same week with a brief." *(Before Monday's call, the brief is written in five lines. Who: the supplier, account number named. What: confirm Thursday delivery of the named product, in the named quantity. Good outcome: Thursday, the right product, the agreed price, a reference number. May agree: confirm the Thursday window, ask for the driver's name. Never agree: a different product, a different price, a different day, without calling the owner first. Introduction: the caller says who they are calling for and that the call is being noted.)* The call takes the same twenty minutes on hold. The supplier offers the substitute product. The caller says, thank you, I will write that down exactly and the owner will call back, and does not accept it. The summary that comes back reads: "Supplier offered substitute product at a higher price for Wednesday. Not accepted. Owner to call back by Tuesday noon. Reference number named." The owner calls Tuesday, agrees the original product for Thursday, and the crew's Thursday job runs.
3. "Here is the record doing its second job." *(The permit call's summary names the reference number, the name of the person spoken to, and the follow-up date. On Friday, when the office is asked for the number, it is in the file, next to the supplier summary, in the place the next person looks first. The call is over, but the record is still working.)* "A brief that took five minutes saved a wrong delivery. A summary filed in the right place saved a second search. Neither required new software. Both required writing things down before and after."
4. "Here is the limit catching a deal." *(A caller is offered a year of a service at a discount if they agree on the call. The never-agree list says: no new service, no contract change, without the owner. The caller writes the offer down word for word, including the price, the term, and the name of the person offering it, and brings it back. The owner reads it, checks it, and declines it, because the business does not need that service.)* "The caller's restraint is the product. A caller who accepts every offer is not helpful. A caller who brings every offer back, written down, is."
5. "Here is the review gate." *(The summary arrives with one fact that looks wrong: a delivery time that conflicts with a turnover already on the calendar. The owner catches it, calls back, and corrects it before Thursday. If the summary had filed itself, the crew would have found the conflict on site.)* "Reading the record is not paperwork after the job. Reading the record is the job."

*Facilitator note:* if the room says their helper cannot make calls yet, answer with the shape point: "Start with a person making the call from a written brief, and a summary filed in the right place. The brief, the limits, and the record work with a notebook, a shared file, or any helper you already pay for. Add a tool later, when the habit is real and the summaries are piling up in a good way."

### 28-33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you will get, and the answers:

- "What if the person on the other end refuses to talk to a helper?": "Then the call ends politely, the summary says so, and a person makes the call from the same brief. A refused call is still a useful outcome, because the brief and the record already exist. The goal was never to force a conversation. The goal was to get the fact, the reference number, or the confirmed time, and to know what happened when you tried."
- "What about recording and telling the other person they are talking to a helper?": "That is a real question, and the honest answer is that the rules vary by where you do business, and the product's public materials do not yet answer it fully. Until you have a clear rule you trust, write your own into the brief: how the caller introduces itself, what it says if asked whether it is an automated caller, and which kinds of calls it simply does not make for you yet. If you cannot write that line honestly, do not delegate that call yet."
- "How much time does the brief take?": "About five minutes for a routine call, and the first brief for a repeated call is the only one you have to write from scratch. The next time the same call comes up, copy the old brief and change the date, the names, and the numbers. Compare that with the cost in the demo: a wrong product, a wrong price, and a lost reference number, all from two calls made without a brief. The brief is cheap on ordinary days and very cheap on the day it catches a deal you did not approve."

### 33-58 · Hand off one real call from your list

Everyone works solo or in pairs on one real call from their own list (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "Read me the call you chose. Who is it to, and what one fact would make it a success?"
- "Read your good outcome sentence. Could a stranger tell whether it happened?"
- "Read your never-agree list out loud. Is a price change on it? A date change? A new service?"
- "How does your caller introduce itself? Say the sentence."
- "Show me where the reference number, the name, and the callback date will go in your summary."
- "Which promises from this call will become tasks, and who owns each one?"
- "Where will the summary live, so the next person finds it before the next call, not after?"
- "What would make you refuse to delegate this call? Write that line too. Some calls stay yours, and naming them is part of the skill."

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their never-agree line and the first promise they will check in the summary, in thirty seconds. "My caller may agree to ___. My caller must never agree to ___. The first promise I will check is ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**The failure gallery.** "The brief that said 'sort it out' and nothing else, so the caller sorted it out beautifully into a deal nobody wanted. The summary that said 'all good' with no names, no numbers, and no date, so the next person had to make the call again. The caller that accepted a discount with a twelve month term, because nobody had written 'never agree to a new service' down. The transcript nobody read, so a wrong delivery time sat in the file until the crew found it on site. The record filed in a private inbox, where the next person never looked. Every one of these is prevented by a rule you used today: a written brief, a may-agree list, a never-agree list, a record a person reads, and promises turned into owned tasks. None of them is prevented by buying a more patient caller."

### 58-60 · Wrap-up and homework

Close in two minutes:

1. Restate the promise kept: "You now hold a brief for one real call: who to call, what to ask, a good outcome in one sentence, what may be agreed, what must never be agreed, how the caller introduces itself, and where the summary will live, with every promise turned into a dated task with a named owner."
2. Homework (say it exactly): "This week, delegate one low-stakes call from your list, from a written brief. Make the call, or have it made. Read the transcript and the summary. Turn every promise into a dated task with a named owner, and file the summary where the next person will find it. Bring the brief and the summary, with the one thing the summary caught, to your next session."
3. Final line: "The call is not the work. The brief before it and the record after it are. Write both, and the phone stops being the place your business loses track of itself."

## Facilitator notes

- Keep all language plain. "Never-agree list" and "may-agree list" may be used only with examples, as they are given in this plan and on the handout.
- Do not present any product named in the news as approved or recommended. Grok Bot calling is an announced capability with open questions about plans, countries, caller ID, and disclosure, and it should be described that way. The brief, the limits, and the record are the deliverable; the products are the examples.
- Treat every vendor number in the source stories as a claim the maker or the vendor made about itself, and say so when you use one. The voice speed and benchmark figures are xAI's figures, and the Hagglebot efficiency figure is Musk's stated belief, not a measured result.
- Time discipline: protect the 25-minute build block. Cut Q&A before cutting building time.
