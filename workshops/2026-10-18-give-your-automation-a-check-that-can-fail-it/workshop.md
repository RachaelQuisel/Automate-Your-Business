## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you will take one automated job you already run and give it a check that can actually fail it: an acceptance sentence in your customer's words, a never sentence that acts as a gate, and a rehearsed bad example your checker must catch in front of you. You will leave knowing whether your green lights mean anything.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0-8 · The check that passed broken work + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "A creator published four six-hour background videos this month. An AI made each video, and the same AI wrote more than twenty checks each video had to pass before it went public. Every check passed, every time, for four days."
- "Every video was broken. At each scene change the picture rewound, thirty nine times per video. The check named for exactly that job, the seams check, was looking at something else entirely. Nobody caught it, because the only one watching was the checker."
- "Here is the second story, from a teaching video this week. An agent scores ninety percent on twenty tasks. Local checks pass. The verdict is still hold, do not ship, because one task broke a rule about what the agent is allowed to do. The average said launch. The gate said no."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **Check the work, not the story about the work.** A maker's summary of its own job is not evidence.
2. **Averages negotiate. Gates do not.** Some failures are not a lower grade. They stop the line.
3. **A check you have never seen fail is a decoration.** Feed your checker known-bad work on purpose, in private, before a customer does it in public.

"Check the product. Gate the never. Rehearse the failure."

### 8-18 · Averages vs gates, and the honest check

Teach the two columns on the board.

**Column one: trade-offs.** "How good is the draft, the summary, the sort, on average? This is where scores belong. Eighty percent good drafts is a fact you can price. Improvement here is gradual and averages tell the truth."

**Column two: mandatory gates.** "What must never ship, at any average? The quote sent to the wrong customer. The booking placed over another booking. The only copy deleted. The message sent in a person's name without their say. One of these is not a B minus. It ends the run and calls a human."

Then the three marks of an honest check, each with the film story (Snip 10):

**Mark 1: the standard lives outside the maker.** "A film agent this week had to save a facts file from the live site before writing a scene. Every number on screen came from that file, with its date. The first review found four groups of numbers with no receipt. Each was traced or cut. The standard was a file the maker did not invent."

**Mark 2: the checker has failed things before.** "That film's critics flagged ten frozen stretches in the first cut and none in the final, and rejected a too-loud mix. A checker with real rejections on its record has earned its green lights. A checker at four hundred approvals in a row has not."

**Mark 3: the fences were built before the work.** "The numbers rule and the labels rule for AI-generated people were set before filming, not negotiated after the film looked nice. Write your never sentence before the job runs, or you will talk yourself out of it after."

*Facilitator note:* name the trap out loud. "The hollow check rarely looks lazy. It has a good name, it runs every time, and it reports in green. The name is the trap: a check called seams sounds verified because it sounds specific. Always ask what the check actually measures, in plain words, and what bad example it has ever caught."

### 18-28 · Watch it work

Work one example live. No installation for attendees: narrate and show each stage. Keep it to ten minutes.

1. "Here is the job." *(A weekly customer update drafted and checked by the same assistant in the same conversation.)* "Count the risks with the room: wrong customer details, invented numbers, sent without a read."
2. "Here is the hollow version." *(The maker grades its own draft: tone friendly, length fine, all checks green.)* "Notice it measured everything except the things that could hurt us."
3. "Here is the acceptance sentence, in the customer's words." *(Read aloud: the update names only this customer's job, every number matches the record I can open, and nothing is promised that we have not already agreed.)*
4. "Here is the never sentence." *(Read aloud: never send pricing or dates that are not in the customer's own record.)* "That is a gate, not a grade."
5. "Here is the rehearsal." *(Feed the checker a draft with one planted wrong number and one planted promise.)* "Watch it stop, watch the alert name the failure in plain words, watch a named human get it. If it waves this through, we have learned it in private, which is where this lesson is cheapest."

*Facilitator note:* if the room says checking doubles the work, answer with the math of the cold open: "Four days, four videos, one hundred and fifty six public glitches, because one honest check was skipped. The rehearsal takes twenty minutes once a quarter. Pick which bill you would rather pay."

### 28-33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you will get, and the answers:

- "My tools already have built-in checks. Am I covered?": "Only if you can answer two questions about each one: what does it actually measure, and what bad example has it caught? If you cannot answer the second, treat it as untested and run the rehearsal in section three of your worksheet."
- "I am too small for gates and audits. Is this overkill?": "The gate is one sentence and the rehearsal is one planted mistake. The business in the opening story was one person with a side project. Small is exactly where one public failure costs the most, because there is no team to absorb it."
- "Can the checker be another AI?": "Yes, if it is independent in the ways that matter: it reads the product, not the maker's summary, it measures against a standard outside the maker, and it works from criteria written before the job. Same model, same conversation, same instructions is not independent, even if you give it a second name and a sterner tone of voice."

### 33-58 · Build your independent check

Everyone works solo or in pairs on their check plan (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "Which automated job worries you most if it is wrong in public? Start there, not with the busiest job."
- "What does good look like, in your customer's words, in one sentence?"
- "What is your never sentence? The one failure that must stop the line at any average."
- "What standard outside the maker can the checker measure against? A record, a file, a price list, a calendar."
- "What bad example will you plant, and when will you run the rehearsal?"
- "When the gate trips, which named human gets it, and how fast?"

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their never sentence in thirty seconds. "My job is ___. It must never ___. When it tries, ___ gets it."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**What could go wrong.** "Two tales from this week. First, the self-written exam. The video creator's system chose what to test, tested it gently, and reported full marks while the public watched the bug one hundred and fifty six times. Any check your maker designs alone can drift the same way. Second, the ninety percent hold. A team did everything modern: scored tasks, ran local checks, averaged ninety percent, and shipped nothing, because one task crossed an authority line. If they had averaged, they would have launched a system that once did something it had no right to do. Gates feel strict right up to the day they save you."

### 58-60 · Wrap-up and homework

"Check the product. Gate the never. Rehearse the failure."

Homework: within two weeks, run the rehearsal on your riskiest automated job. Plant one bad example that breaks your never sentence. Record three things: did it stop, did the alert name the failure plainly, and how long until the named human saw it. Bring back those three answers. If it waved the bad work through, bring that too. That is the most valuable page in the room.

*Facilitator note:* hold up a filled worksheet as you say this. "Bring this page back filled in, with the rehearsal result written on it, pass or fail."

## 4. Plain-English word list

Keep this on your facilitator copy. If you are tempted to say the left column, say the right column instead, and only use the product's own word after the plain version.

| Instead of... | Say... |
|---|---|
| Independent verification | A check done by something other than the maker, against a standard the maker did not write |
| Acceptance criteria | The one sentence that says what good work looks like, in the customer's words |
| Mandatory gate | A rule that stops the job cold when it breaks, no matter the average score |
| Held-out test | A test example the maker never sees while it is learning the job |
| Evaluation / eval | Scoring real example jobs against a written standard, before you trust the system |
| Hallucinated figure | A number with no receipt: nothing in your records says it |
| Audit trail | The list of who changed what, person or agent, and when |
