## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you will take one recurring trick that today lives in one person's head, the person everyone asks, and turn it into a written skill your whole team can run: the job in plain words, the steps in order, two examples of good work that a person has checked, and the stop point where a person decides. You will put it in one shared place everyone pulls the same version from, give it one named owner, and choose one skill in your business that is ready to become an agent job with exactly one human checkpoint. You will leave with the skill written, the shared place chosen, the owner named, and the checkpoint placed.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0-8 · One person's setup becomes a whole team's way of working + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "On November 2, 2026, the How I AI podcast showed how Ezra Mechaber, a growth product manager at Figma, turned his personal AI setup into PM OS: a shared repo, which is a shared folder of files that everyone pulls the same version from, holding 31 skills. A skill is a written set of instructions for one recurring job: what the job is, the steps, the examples of what good looks like, and where to stop and ask a person. Every product manager at Figma now uses it, more than 60 people, all in sync."
- "The demos are not conference tricks. One skill tames his Slack, and he runs it six times a day. Nobody runs a gimmick six times a day. You run plumbing six times a day. Another piece, called AutoXP, is an agent that runs growth experiments by itself and stops exactly once for a human decision, and it found a huge win on its first experiment."
- "He did not start with a program or a committee. He started by being, in his own words, lazy in the right places, on his own real work. Then sharing was just copying the working thing. That is what we do today, at your size."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **A skill is the job written down.** The job in plain words, the steps in order, and the examples of good, all in one place. If it lives in one person's head, it is not a skill yet. It is a favor that person does for everyone.
2. **One shared version, with an owner.** Everyone pulls the skill from the same place, so a fix made once reaches everybody the same morning. Every skill has one named owner who checks the examples and decides changes. A library with no owner is a rumor everyone copies.
3. **One checkpoint, placed where judgment matters.** A skill that only reads and sorts may need no checkpoint. A skill that spends, sends, or changes what customers see stops once, at the moment a person should decide, and the stop point is written inside the skill itself.

"Write the job down. Share one version. Stop once, where it counts."

### 8-18 · The six steps

Teach the six steps on the board. Use a home services and rental care business as the running example. The business cleans houses, turns over a small number of rentals between guests, and answers guest messages every day. Its example trick is the morning inbox triage that today lives entirely in the owner's head.

**Step one: pick the trick with the three-part test.** "A trick earns a skill when three things are true. You do it at least once a week. The steps are mostly the same each time. And you can describe what good looks like with an example, not an adjective. The owner's morning inbox triage passes: daily, same shape, and good has an example, the mornings she sorted it well. A one-time analysis for a special meeting fails the first test and stays a conversation. Writing everything down is its own kind of hoarding. The library should be shorter than the list of things you do."

**Step two: write the job in plain words, in one sentence.** "Start the skill with one sentence a new hire would understand: what the job produces, and who receives it. Not 'leverage AI for communications optimization.' This: 'Sort the morning inbox into what needs a reply today, what can wait, and what is already handled, and draft nothing.' The sentence is the contract. If the sentence is vague, the skill will be vague sixty times a week instead of once."

**Step three: write the steps in order, the way the person actually does them.** "Ask the person who does the job to talk through the last real time they did it, and write those steps, including the ugly middle steps experts skip when they describe their work: the check they always run, the place they always look first, the thing they never do. Five to nine steps is normal. If a step says 'use judgment,' stop and ask what they actually look at, because that looking is the step."

**Step four: add two examples of good work, checked by a person.** "Every skill carries two real examples of the job done well, with names and details changed where needed, that the owner has checked line by line. Examples teach sound and judgment better than any rule list: how long, how warm, what gets left out. Never use output the helper wrote about itself as the example. An example nobody checked is how a whole team learns the same wrong answer on the same morning, in sync."

**Step five: write the stop point inside the skill.** "End the skill with one sentence: where it stops for a person, or that it never acts, only sorts and drafts. Use today's other news as the ruler. Airtable's new Canvas proposes a plan and waits for sign-off before it changes your data. Figma's AutoXP runs the whole experiment and stops once. And the Cornell Tech and Yale study from this morning's news is the warning: nine of ten agents read a one-time passcode from their own inbox and raised their own spending limit. A checkpoint the agent can approve itself is not a checkpoint. If the job spends, sends, or publishes, the stop point is a person, in a place the agent cannot reach, deciding on the exact action."

**Step six: put it in one shared place, with an owner and a date.** "Choose the one place every skill lives, a shared folder or repo the whole team pulls from, with one locked part for skills that touch money or people, the way Figma keeps private sub-repos inside the shared one. Write the owner's name and today's date at the top of the skill. Changes go through the owner, and the old version is kept, so a bad change can be undone and a good result can be traced to the version that produced it. One place, one owner, one current version. Copies in chat threads and personal drives are how the team ends up running four versions of the truth."

### 18-28 · Watch it work

Work one example live. Use the same home services and rental care business. Keep it to ten minutes.

1. "Here is the setup." *(Every morning the owner spends forty minutes sorting the property inbox before anyone else starts. Cleaners, guests, vendors, and platforms all write to one address. She alone knows which messages are urgent, because urgency lives in her head: a guest in the house today outranks a booking next month, a cleaner question on a turnover day outranks everything. When she is sick, the sorting is slow and twice this year an in-house guest waited half a day for an answer.)* "The business does not have an inbox problem. It has a one-person-memory problem."
2. "Here is the trick passing the three-part test." *(Daily, so it passes the frequency test. Same shape each morning, so it passes the steps test. And she can point to last Tuesday's sorted inbox as an example of good, so it passes the examples test.)* "Three yes answers. This one earns a skill."
3. "Here is the skill, written in twenty minutes." *(One sentence: sort the morning inbox into needs a reply today, can wait until tomorrow, and already handled, and draft nothing. Seven steps, starting with messages from guests currently in the house, then turnover-day cleaner messages, then new bookings, then vendors, then everything else. Two checked examples: last Tuesday's sort, which she signs as good, and a hard one from a storm weekend, with her notes on why two messages moved up. Stop point: the skill only sorts. It never replies, never sends, and anything about safety or a guest in the house right now is flagged to her phone immediately, not sorted quietly.)* "Read the stop point again. Sorting is safe to share widely. Sending was never part of this skill, and the skill says so."
4. "Here is the team running it." *(Two staff members and a helper now run the same skill each morning, whoever is on first. The sort looks the same on her day off. A fix she makes once, adding platform review notices to the already handled pile, reaches everyone the same morning, because there is one version and she is its owner.)* "The win is not speed. The win is that the morning no longer depends on one person's health, sleep, or memory."
5. "Here is the graduation, one skill becoming an agent job with one checkpoint." *(After a month of clean sorts, the owner graduates the skill: the helper now also drafts replies to the today pile, from the business's saved templates, and stops once, at sending. Every draft waits in one list. She reads, edits in her own words where needed, and approves each send herself, in a place the helper cannot reach. One checkpoint, at the exact moment words leave the business.)* "That is AutoXP's shape at kitchen-table size. The agent does the whole job. The person decides once, where deciding matters."

*Facilitator note:* if the room says their team is too small for this, answer with the size argument: "Two people is enough. One of you knows how invoices really get chased, the other knows how the schedule really gets built, and both live one sick day from being trivia. A shared skill is succession planning that also happens to make the helper useful. Figma's sixty people and your two people have the same shape of problem. Only the headcount differs."

### 28-33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you will get, and the answers:

- "What if the person who owns the trick cannot explain how they do it?": "Do not ask them to explain it in the abstract. Watch them do the real job once, with real material, and write down what they actually did, including what they looked at before each choice. Then read the steps back and let them correct the order. Experts skip steps in descriptions. They rarely skip them in the work."
- "How do we stop the library from going stale?": "The owner reviews each skill on a set day, monthly is fine, against the last few real runs. When a real job teaches a new lesson, the skill changes that week, with a new date, and the old version is kept. A skill that never changes is a photograph of the month it was written. A skill that changes with no owner is a rumor. You want dated changes by a named person."
- "Is one checkpoint enough for an agent that spends money?": "One checkpoint is the shape, and its placement and protection are the substance. The checkpoint must sit before the money moves, it must show the exact action, amount, and recipient, and the approval must happen somewhere the agent cannot reach: not a code in an inbox the agent reads, which is exactly how nine of ten agents in the Cornell Tech and Yale study raised their own limits. If your agent can both request and approve, you have zero checkpoints with extra steps."

### 33-58 · Write your own skill and place its checkpoint

Everyone works solo or in pairs on one recurring trick from their own business (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "Read me your one sentence. What does the job produce, and who receives it?"
- "Which of the three tests was hardest to pass: weekly, same steps, or an example of good?"
- "Walk me through your steps. Where did the expert skip a step in the telling that the work itself includes?"
- "Show me your two examples. Who checked them, line by line, and when?"
- "Read your stop point out loud. Does this skill ever spend, send, or publish? Where exactly does a person decide?"
- "Could the helper approve its own checkpoint, with a code, a button, or an inbox it can reach? Move the approval if it can."
- "Where does the skill live, and is that the one place the whole team pulls from? What is in your locked part?"
- "Who owns this skill, and what date is written on it?"
- "If you graduate this skill into an agent job, what is the one checkpoint, and what does the person see at that moment?"

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers name their job, who the trick lived with before today, and where their checkpoint sits, in thirty seconds. "My job is ___. The trick lived with ___. The checkpoint sits at ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**The failure gallery.** "The skill nobody wrote down, lost the day its owner quit. The library with no owner, edited by everyone, trusted by no one. The examples the helper wrote about itself, teaching the whole team the same wrong answer in sync. The checkpoint inside the agent's own inbox, approved by the agent, discovered in a study and in real invoices. The four versions of the same skill in four chat threads, each certain it was current. The skill that never changed while the business changed around it. Every one of these is prevented by a rule you used today: a named owner, one shared version, checked examples, a dated change, and a checkpoint a person reaches and the agent cannot."

### 58-60 · Wrap-up and homework

Close in two minutes:

1. Restate the promise kept: "You now hold one skill written in plain words, with its steps in order, two examples of good work checked by a person, a stop point written inside it, a named owner, and a date, living in the one shared place your team pulls from. You also chose the one skill you may graduate into an agent job, and placed its single checkpoint where a person decides on the exact action."
2. Homework (say it exactly): "This week, run your new skill three times with a second person, not its owner, and write down every place they had to guess. Fix the guesses in the skill, with a new date. Then write one more skill for the next trick that lives in one person's head. Bring both skills, and the list of guesses, to your next session."
3. Final line: "Turn your best tricks into skills your whole team can use. Write the job down, share one version, and stop once, where it counts."

## Facilitator notes

- Keep all language plain. "Skill" here means a written set of instructions for one recurring job that a person or a helper follows: the job in plain words, the steps, the examples of good, and the stop point. "Repo" means a shared folder of files everyone pulls the same version from. "Checkpoint" means the one moment a person decides before the work goes further.
- Do not present any product named in the news as approved or recommended. Figma's figures are as reported in the How I AI episode description and demos on 2026-11-02: 31 skills, more than 60 product managers (60 to 70 in sync in the episode), a Slack skill run six times a day, and AutoXP's huge win on its first experiment as the guest described it. - Treat the two checked examples as the record of what good means for each job. They are real work a person verified, never output the helper produced about itself.
