# Would It Have Happened Anyway?

**Title:** Would It Have Happened Anyway?

**Audience:** Small business owners and operators: no technical background assumed

## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you will turn one recurring thing your business makes into a result loop with guarded edges: you will name the one true signal that tells you it worked, fence off a small holdout the loop never touches so that signal can be checked against reality, and write the change rule that says what the loop may adjust on its own and what waits for a named person.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0-8 · The loop that graded its own homework + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "On October 23, 2026, a platform called Cuttable announced an update built around a complete loop. It reads a brand's website and guidelines, turns a plain-language brief into finished ads, publishes them, reads the results back, and writes the next brief from what worked. Across 280,000 ads and more than $320 million in advertising spend on the platform, one ad in eleven returned ten times its cost or better."
- "Here is the catch, and it is the whole workshop. The number the loop learns from is the number the advertising platform reports, and that number takes credit for sales that would have happened anyway. A customer who had already put the item in their cart sees an ad, buys the item, and the report says the ad did it. Round after round, the loop gets better at selling to people who were already buying, and worse at finding new people, while the report applauds."
- "Today you will build the honest version of that loop, at your size, for one thing you already make every week."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **The signal decides what the loop makes more of.** If the signal rewards work that was going to succeed anyway, the loop produces more of that work, faster, and calls it improvement.
2. **The holdout is where the truth lives.** A small group, week, or area the loop never touches is the only way to know what would have happened if you had done nothing.
3. **The edges are yours.** A person guards what goes into the loop and what the loop may change. The middle can run on its own. The edges cannot.

"The true signal. The fenced holdout. The written change rule."

### 8-18 · The three parts of a Result Loop

Teach the three parts on the board. Use one weekly email from a home services business with twelve staff as the running example.

**Part one: the one true signal.** "Pick the one number that tells you the work worked, and test it with this question: would this number still look good if I had done nothing? Email opens fail that test, because people open email from businesses they already use. Replies that turn into booked work pass it, because booked work either happened or it did not. For ads, the reported return fails the test on its own. The return counted only against people who were not already about to buy passes it. Write the signal as a full sentence, with what it counts and who counts it. A signal nobody can count the same way twice is not a signal. It is a mood."

**Part two: the fenced holdout.** "Choose a small part of the world the loop never touches: one week in four when the sequence does not run, one neighborhood that gets no flyers this month, one group of customers in ten who are left out of the campaign. Small enough that you can afford it, large enough that its results mean something. The roundup of the Cuttable launch did this arithmetic in the open: on a $100,000 month, holding back ten percent costs about $10,000 in held-back ads and tells you whether the reported return of 4.4 times is real, because in its worked example the corrected return was 2.42 times. Still profitable. Completely different lesson. Then write the fence rule: the name of the one person who may open the fence, on a calm day, in writing. The first bad week is when everyone wants to spend the holdout to fix the month. The fence exists for exactly that week."

**Part three: the written change rule.** "A loop changes something between rounds. Write down what it may change on its own, and what waits for a person. A workable starting rule: the loop may change wording, timing, and order inside rules a person wrote. The loop may not change the offer, the price, the audience, or the signal. Those four wait for a named person. Add the review rhythm: a named person reads the loop's changes and the holdout comparison at a set time each week. This is the bank lesson. Customers Bank, which closed select commercial loans in seven days instead of thirty to sixty, runs every new agent through one cycle: shadow the real workflow, build on the real data, measure the result before the agent joins daily work. The measurement step is not caution. The measurement step is where the speed comes from."

Then the habit that keeps it honest:

**The queue rule.** "If your loop also recommends actions, like the Navos Pro agent launched the same week, which watches data continuously and queues recommendations, then the queue is a job. Every recommendation lands in one of three piles: act now, decide this week at the review time, or ignore on purpose, written down. A queue nobody reads costs the same as no agent at all."

*Facilitator note:* name the trap out loud. "The failure is not a loop that learns slowly. The failure is a loop that learns the wrong lesson very fast, with beautiful charts. Every story in the failure gallery is a signal somebody did not question on a calm day."

### 18-28 · Watch it work

Work one example live. Use a printed email report and a printed loop page. Keep it to ten minutes.

1. "Here is the reported way." *(The weekly email goes to the full customer list. Opens are strong. The owner makes more emails like the one with the highest opens, which was sent to the most loyal customers before a holiday weekend.)* "Nobody here is bad at email. The signal was never questioned, so every round teaches the same lesson: send loyal people more email before holidays."
2. "Here is the same email as a result loop." *(The signal is replies that turn into booked work. One week in four is fenced: no email. The loop may change the subject line and the send time. The offer and the audience wait for the owner.)*
3. "Here is the holdout comparison." *(In the fenced weeks, booked work from past customers dips a little, not a lot. Most of the email's reported power was habit. The real lift is smaller and real, and it comes from one segment: customers who had not booked in a year.)* "The loop now has a true job: win back the quiet customers. That job was invisible in the opens report."
4. "Here is the change rule test." *(The loop proposes a discount to lift replies.)* "The rule answers in one second: the offer waits for a named person. The loop may test a new subject line today. The discount waits for the owner on Friday."
5. "Here is the fence test." *(A slow week arrives and a staff member wants to send the email in the fenced week to catch up.)* "The fence rule answers: one named person may open it, on a calm day, in writing. A slow Tuesday is not a calm day."

*Facilitator note:* if the room says their work is too small for a holdout, answer with the size point: "A holdout does not need to be large. It needs to be untouched. One week in four costs you three weeks of a small lift you have not measured yet, and buys you the only honest number in the building."

### 28-33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you will get, and the answers:

- "What if my holdout shows the work does almost nothing?": "Then the loop just paid for itself in one month. You were about to scale work that does almost nothing, at three times the output. The holdout did not kill your marketing. It told you to move the effort to the segment or the offer that the comparison shows actually moves."
- "Is a holdout not just leaving money on the table?": "It leaves a measured amount on a small table, for a set time, to protect the large table. The worked example from this week's news put the test at ten percent of one month's spend. Compare that with the cost of a year of decisions made from a number that credits sales you would have made anyway."
- "What does it cost me?": "One hour today, one fenced week or group you protect, and one short review at a set time each week. The loop tool, if you use one, costs whatever it costs. The honesty is free, and it is the part that decides whether the tool was worth buying."

### 33-58 · Build your Result Loop page

Everyone works solo or in pairs on their loop page (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "Read me your recurring work. One thing, made on a schedule, not a one-time project."
- "What is your one true signal? Say what it counts, and tell me how it would look if you had done nothing."
- "What is fenced, and for how long? Who is the one person who may open the fence?"
- "Read your change rule out loud. What may the loop change alone, and which four things wait: offer, price, audience, signal?"
- "When is the weekly review, and whose name is on it?"
- "If your loop queues recommendations, what are your three piles?"

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their true signal and their holdout in thirty seconds. "My loop makes ___. My true signal is ___. My holdout is ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**The failure gallery.** "The ad account that looked more efficient every quarter while new customers quietly dried up, because the loop learned to harvest people at the checkout. The email program that sent more and more to the happiest customers until they unsubscribed from fatigue. The recommendation queue that reached forty unread items and became a guilt machine the owner muted. The month the holdout was spent to fix a slow week, and the business never got its honest number back. Every one of these is prevented by a sentence in the three parts. None of them is prevented by a better model next quarter."

### 58-60 · Wrap-up and homework

Close in two minutes:

1. Restate the promise kept: "You now hold a one-page loop that names its true signal, fences its holdout, and writes what may change on its own and what waits for a person."
2. Homework (say it exactly): "This week, pick the biggest number in the report you already believe, and write down how much of it would have happened if you had done nothing. Then set your first holdout dates on the calendar and protect them. Bring the comparison to your next session."
3. Final line: "Feed the loop the truth, or the loop will feed on the flattering number. Choose the truth while the choice is still cheap."

## Facilitator notes

- Keep all language plain. "Return on ad spend" may be used only with its one-sentence explanation: the revenue a platform credits to an ad for each dollar spent on it.
- Do not present any product named in the news as approved or recommended. The loop page is the deliverable; the products are the examples.
- Treat every vendor number in the source story as a claim the vendor made about itself, and say so when you use one. The worked holdout example uses stated assumptions, not a merchant's private results.
- Time discipline: protect the 25-minute build block. Cut Q&A before cutting building time.
