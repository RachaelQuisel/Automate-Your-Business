## Idea 1: The fifth part of a loop most people skip ⭐ strongest (carousel built)

- **Episode:** Lenny's Podcast, "Why companies are becoming a series of loops", Anish Acharya (a16z)
- **Snips used:** #5 (Software work is becoming interconnected loops), #6 (Companies will become cascading AI loops), #8 (Kavak agents learn from human rescue calls)
- **What the snips say:** Anish described AI agents turning recurring workflows into loops that run with little human help. His example: a bug report can trigger reproduction, a fix, review, deployment and a customer email in five minutes (#5). Humans stay essential for sales, strategy, exceptions, and deciding what's worth building (#6). Kavak's customer agents call a human whenever they get stuck. The human's coaching resolves the case and leaves traces that help the agent avoid the same mistake (#8).
- **Key insight in plain words:** a useful automation doesn't just run steps. It knows when it's stuck and who to ask, and getting rescued makes it better next time.
- **How I'm applying it:** I turned this episode into my first *Automate Your Business* workshop, "The Loop Audit". It names five parts of a loop: trigger, steps, decision, output, escape hatch. Most people skip the fifth. I'm using that checklist on my own daily staff text for La Maison. Its trigger is a day someone's scheduled. Its decision is whether a guest is arriving (name it, or say "No guest checking in"). Its output is one text. The escape hatch is the part I'm still designing.
- **Best audience:** **both**. Hosts get a checklist they can use. Hiring managers see product judgment about failure modes and humans in the loop.

## Idea 2: Build one small AI project a week

- **Episode:** same episode, Anish Acharya
- **Snip used:** #14 (Ship one AI project every week)
- **What the snip says:** product people should build a small project every week to build intuition with new models. The project can be unimportant. His example was a personalized Mother's Day presentation made from messages and photos.
- **Key insight in plain words:** you learn what AI can do by building small things often, not by reading about it.
- **How I'm applying it:** this post comes out of one of those small projects. Snipd emails me my podcast snips. A scheduled agent picks one episode on the 1st and 15th of each month and drafts a workshop from it. One design choice I care about: it saves the snips **verbatim** in one file and writes the workshop in another. The record stays separate from the interpretation, and it's told not to invent a quote, stat or link. My GitHub also has a row of small Claude Code plugins I've shipped lately (questions-worth-asking, humanize-output, turn-off-ai-data-sharing, and more).
- **Best audience:** **hiring managers** first, founders second.

## Idea 3: People who "aren't technical" can build agents too

- **Episode:** same episode, Anish Acharya
- **Snip used:** #3 (AI adoption starts before company reorganization)
- **What the snip says:** employees are more eager to use AI for leverage than the stereotype of resistant workers suggests. Kavak trained mechanics for six weeks, and each one ended up shipping a production agent.
- **Key insight in plain words:** the people closest to the work can build useful AI tools when the training meets them where they are.
- **How I'm applying it:** my *Automate Your Business* workshops assume no technical background. I keep a plain-English word list: say "saved instructions (like a recipe card)" instead of "skill", "the start signal" instead of "trigger". My facilitator rule: if I catch myself saying "API" or "agentic workflow," I restart the sentence. Hosts, cleaners and small business owners know their workflows better than any consultant does.
- **Best audience:** **hosts** first; hiring managers see enablement and teaching skills.

## Idea 4: Design the expensive version first

- **Episode:** same episode, Anish Acharya
- **Snip used:** #13 (Design the expensive version first)
- **What the snip says:** question the assumption that consumer products have to be cheap or free. Picture a hypothetical $1,000 or $10,000-a-month version to see what exceptional value would take.
- **Key insight in plain words:** imagining the premium version shows you what customers value most, and then you can decide which parts to make simple.
- **How I'm applying it:** I ran this exercise on airbnbai.rent. If a host paid for the "everything" version, what would it include? My answer looks like the three steps already on my site: someone **listens** to your week (listing, calendar, messages, turnovers) and writes up findings; someone **builds** it and saves earlier versions you can restore if something breaks; and you get a **handover** you keep (prompt libraries, recorded walkthroughs, written playbooks, agreed support). The exercise showed me the expensive part is the listening and the handover, not the AI. **[confirm this conclusion is Rachael's]**
- **Best audience:** **both**. Founders and hiring managers see product and pricing thinking; hosts see what good help looks like.

## Idea 5: Happier, not just more productive

- **Episode:** same episode, Anish Acharya
- **Snips used:** #9 (The biggest consumer opportunity is "make me happier"), with #12 (Word of mouth is the new network effect) as a supporting line
- **What the snips say:** the biggest consumer opportunity may be helping people feel happier, more connected, healthier and more fulfilled, not just more productive. Anish frames that as a product-design challenge, not a model-capability one (#9). As platforms limit what startups can build on their networks, word of mouth becomes a key moat, so products need to be remarkable enough that people mention them (#12).
- **Key insight in plain words:** measure tools by whether they give people their lives back, not just by tasks done. When people feel that, they tell others.
- **How I'm applying it:** the promise on airbnbai.rent isn't "be more productive". It's *recover time in your week to do more of what you want.* After 11 years of hosting La Maison (659 guest reviews, 4.97 average), my weekly workload is about 5 hours. For hosts, reviews are word of mouth. Guests are happier when the host isn't stretched thin.
- **Best audience:** **hosts** first; founders second.
